# Cup Dash: course themes, art direction and making cheap visual variety on mobile

Scope: themes ("maps") for a stylised 3D golf game, what makes each one distinct to look at and to play, art direction that reads on a small portrait screen, and ways to build several distinct courses within mid-range Android budgets (Godot 4.7 Mobile renderer, 60 fps target / 30 fps floor).

Repo context (not web-sourced): docs/games/golf.md fixes the physics levers a theme can change: wind 0-10 m/s per hole (seeded), per-surface bounce (green e = Penner e(v), mu 0.40; fairway e 0.22, mu 0.5), Stimp-based green speed, and water / OB as +1 stroke. docs/plans/2026-11-14-cup-dash.md already sets a budget of "MultiMesh trees, blob shadow, strip-mesh trail, <= 100 draw calls".

Research ran on 2026-11-14 with about 20 search/fetch calls. One fetch failed (an art article on a stylised golf game returned HTTP 403), and I found no art breakdowns of shipped mobile golf games (see Gaps).

## 1. Course themes: how real course types and common game themes differ in gameplay

### Takeaway
Real courses fall into a few types: links, parkland, heathland, desert and mountain, plus clifftop, sandbelt and jungle as extras. The types differ along a few axes: wind, how firm the ground is (roll), what lines the fairway (trees, gorse/heather, sand/rock, dunes), forced carries, bunker depth and elevation. Those axes map almost one-to-one onto the per-hole parameters Cup Dash already has, so a theme can be a parameter preset plus a visual kit.

### Cited Findings
- **Links:** built on sandy ground by the sea, so "cooler temperatures, wind strength and direction and the likelihood of rain" are constant factors. Low, running shots matter because low shots "bounce for days". Hazards are blind shots, gorse and dunes. Bunkers are deeper, "sometimes with revetted faces more than 10 feet high". Bump-and-run and low punch shots are essential — [GolfPass](https://www.golfpass.com/travel-advisor/articles/most-difficult-golf-courses-to-play-links-heathland-parkland-mountain-desert)
- Links is described as the original style, from the Scottish coast. It is the most natural style, with firmer, uneven fairways and more wind than parkland — [Keiser University College of Golf](https://collegeofgolf.keiseruniversity.edu/types-golf-courses-links-parkland-desert); [MyGolfSpy](https://mygolfspy.com/news-opinion/what-is-links-golf/)
- **Parkland:** inland, with trees, ponds and bunkers. The GolfPass author calls it "generally one-dimensional". It has narrow fairways, knee-high rough, and firm fairways and greens that can bounce the ball into trouble. Many courses have removed trees to open driving corridors — [GolfPass](https://www.golfpass.com/travel-advisor/articles/most-difficult-golf-courses-to-play-links-heathland-parkland-mountain-desert). Other summaries describe parkland as heavily shaped by architects, tree-lined, with water hazards and softer, flatter fairways (Augusta National is the usual example) — [Your Golf Travel](https://yourgolftravel.com/articles/types-of-golf-courses); [Golfbreaks](https://www.golfbreaks.com/en-gb/inspiration/articles/guide-to-different-types-of-golf-courses/). *The sources disagree on firmness: GolfPass says parkland fairways and greens are firm, while the travel and summary sites say soft.*
- **Heathland:** inland on sandy, well-draining ground covered in heather and gorse, with "firm, bouncy, sandy terrain" similar to links. "An off-line tee shot into the heather or gorse is the biggest hazard". Fairways are narrow ("U.S. Open-narrow"). Bunkers are deeper and more penal than in parkland, often lined with heather. Recovery often needs a steep chop-out. It also has trees and ponds — [GolfPass](https://www.golfpass.com/travel-advisor/articles/most-difficult-golf-courses-to-play-links-heathland-parkland-mountain-desert). Heathland is described as a hybrid of links and parkland, found mostly in England — [Golf Today](https://golftoday.co.uk/the-different-types-of-golf-courses/); [England iGolf](https://englandigolf.co.uk/igolf-blog/6-different-types-of-golf-courses-explained/)
- **Desert:** dominated by sand, rock and cacti, so the fairways can look narrow. The landscape is "one giant sand trap", and courses have "an abnormal amount of forced carries" off the tee or on the approach — [GolfPass](https://www.golfpass.com/travel-advisor/articles/most-difficult-golf-courses-to-play-links-heathland-parkland-mountain-desert). Lush fairways sit against sandy surroundings, and accuracy matters more than distance because a wayward shot ends up in rocks, sand or native plants — [Keiser University](https://collegeofgolf.keiseruniversity.edu/types-golf-courses-links-parkland-desert); [Your Golf Travel](https://yourgolftravel.com/articles/types-of-golf-courses)
- **Mountain:** usually "above 2,000 feet in elevation", with rugged natural terrain. The main challenge is consistent contact from "uphill, downhill or sidehill lies", especially over ravines. Elevation and slope change how far shots fly and how far they need to go — [GolfPass](https://www.golfpass.com/travel-advisor/articles/most-difficult-golf-courses-to-play-links-heathland-parkland-mountain-desert)
- **How altitude changes distance:** a Titleist study found the ball flies about 1.2% farther per 1,000 ft of elevation (elevation in ft × 0.00116), so roughly 6% at 5,000 ft — [golf.com](https://golf.com/instruction/4-keys-playing-when-playing-at-elevation/); [Titleist](https://www.titleist.com/teamtitleist/gb/b/weblog/posts/the-effect-of-altitude-on-golf-ball-performance). Many calculators use a larger "2% per 1,000 ft" rule (1.5-2.5%) — [MyGolfSpy](https://mygolfspy.com/news-opinion/instruction/golf-distance-chart-for-altitude-how-to-adjust-your-yardages/); [The Golf News Net](https://thegolfnewsnet.com/ryan_ballengee/2024/02/29/how-much-temperature-humidity-elevation-affect-how-far-golf-ball-flies-103715). *The sources conflict. The Titleist number is the better-sourced one.*
- Other named types are sandbelt, clifftop, jungle and moorland, but the sources do not describe them — [GolfPass](https://www.golfpass.com/travel-advisor/articles/most-difficult-golf-courses-to-play-links-heathland-parkland-mountain-desert); [Your Golf Travel](https://yourgolftravel.com/articles/types-of-golf-courses)
- **Themes in a shipped mobile golf game (described generically, named only as a source):** a top-grossing mobile golf PvP game advertises "13 challenging tours" and "dozens of golf courses - new courses added every month" — [Apptopia listing of Golf Clash](https://apptopia.com/google-play/app/com.playdemic.golf.android/about). Its season rotation pages list courses themed on white cliffs, Nordic fjords, a Paris park and coastal links, alongside licensed real courses, and courses move between tours each season — [EA Golf Clash Season 80 rotation](https://ea.com/games/golf-clash/amp/news/season-80-course-rotation); [EA Season 77 rotation](https://ea.com/games/golf-clash/news/season-77-tour-rotation). *The theme list comes from search snippets of those pages, not a full read.*
- Low-poly indie golf games use theming as their main way to vary content. One describes "ever-expanding vibrant, spiritful backdrops" — [SereniTee on itch.io](https://dlynch.itch.io/serenitee). A minigolf tycoon game ships themed course sets such as a medieval corner — [Mini Golf Tycoon devlog](https://ludorem-studios.itch.io/mini-golf-tycoon/devlog/349042/tons-of-features). One stylised golf game uses a single strong narrative setting (post-apocalyptic Earth as a course for the rich) — [Wireframe / Golf Club: Wasteland](https://wireframe.raspberrypi.com/articles/the-art-of-golf-club-wasteland)

### Inferences
Each theme below is a gameplay preset over Cup Dash's existing parameters plus a visual kit. Numbers are suggestions for tuning, not sourced.

| Theme | Gameplay identity | Wind (of 0-10 m/s) | Firmness / roll | Signature hazards | Elevation | Visual signature |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parkland (starter) | Forgiving, aerial target golf; learn the controls | Low, 0-3 | Medium-soft fairway; greens hold | Tree lines, ponds, shallow bunkers | Gentle | Saturated greens, round tree canopies, blue water |
| Links / coastal | Ground game and wind; low running shots pay | High, 4-10 | Firm: fairway e up, mu down, longer roll | Pot bunkers (deep), dunes, gorse, sea OB, blind shots | Rolling humps, few trees | Desaturated olive and straw grass, grey-blue sea, big sky |
| Heathland | Accuracy off the tee; narrow corridors | Medium, 2-5 | Firm, bouncy | Heather/gorse margins (heavy-rough surface), deep heather-lipped bunkers | Moderate | Purple-magenta heather against green; pines |
| Desert / canyon | Forced carries; all-or-nothing; strong contrast | Medium, 2-6 | Firm fairway; anything off it is "waste" (sand/rock = OB or heavy penalty) | Carries over waste and rock, cacti, canyon OB | Mesas, drops | Bright green ribbons on ochre and terracotta; long shadows |
| Alpine / mountain | Slope lies and up/down distance judgement; the ball flies a little farther | Variable, gusty | Medium | Ravines (OB), sidehill lies, streams | Big elevation, downhill tees | Conifers, grey rock, snow caps on the skyline, cool light |
| Tropical island | Island greens and water carries | Medium, 3-6 | Soft, greens hold | Lagoon water, beach sand, palms | Low, with a volcano backdrop | Turquoise water, white sand, palm silhouettes |
| Autumn forest | Shaping shots around trees | Low, 1-3 | Medium; leaf-litter rough slows the ball | Dense tree lines, creeks | Moderate | Orange, red and gold canopies (see the colour-blind note in section 2) |
| Winter / snow (fantasy) | Low friction and slick greens | Low-medium | Fairway rolls far; snow "rough" stops the ball dead | Frozen ponds (could skip like ice), snowbanks | Moderate | White and pale blue palette; needs strong value contrast for the fairway |
| Night / floodlit | Same terrain as another theme, different mood | Low | As the base theme | As the base theme, plus darkness beyond the floodlit zone | As the base theme | Dark sky gradient, pools of light, emissive markers |
| Fantasy (floating isles) | Void as OB everywhere, carries between islands | Medium | Medium | Void edges, bouncy mushrooms (optional) | Extreme | Free palette, sky islands, waterfalls |

- Night and seasonal variants (autumn, snow) of an existing course are the cheapest kind of "new map": same height grid, new palette, sky and props. Shipped mobile golf games show that theme names and rotations alone keep content feeling fresh.
- A suggested unlock order, from gentlest to hardest: parkland, tropical, autumn, heathland, links, desert, alpine, plus a night variant of each.
- Altitude: if alpine uses a carry multiplier, about +1.2% per 1,000 ft (the Titleist figure) is the defensible value. A slightly lower air density (rho) in the flight solver gives the same effect physically.

- No source describes sandbelt, clifftop or jungle courses.
- I could not get a complete, authoritative list of themes from shipped mobile golf games. The EA rotation pages were only seen as snippets.

## 2. Stylised art direction that reads on a small portrait screen

### Takeaway
Use a limited palette per theme, flat or gradient shading, and strong silhouettes. Above all, make fairway, rough, green, sand and water differ in **brightness**, not just hue, because the green/grey/brown pairs that golf depends on are exactly the hardest pairs for colour-vision-deficient players. Flat or gradient palette texturing is also the cheapest approach on mobile (see section 3).

### Cited Findings
- **Limited palettes:** stylised environment artists repeatedly describe restricting the palette for cohesion. One recipe is to pick a local colour, limit the palette, then add a stronger colour accent in chosen areas. Simple colours and gradients "need very little texture space" — [80.lv search summary: Temporal Hub, Viking environment, Stanley Barrett breakdowns](https://80.lv/articles/stylized-production-tips-and-tricks); [80.lv Viking breakdown](https://80.lv/articles/breakdown-building-lived-in-stylized-viking-environment-in-3d); [80.lv Creating visual interest in low poly](https://80.lv/articles/creating-visual-interest-in-low-poly-artworks)
- **Gradients for depth:** "a gradient of gradients", built from colour layers, AO and curvature — [80.lv Drunk Lake Inn breakdown](https://80.lv/articles/the-drunk-lake-inn-stylized-environment-breakdown). Put a gradient on each building rather than across the whole city — [80.lv search summary (Benjamin Lucas)](https://80.lv/articles/stylized-look-in-environment-design). Remap grunge maps through a gradient filter onto stylised palettes — [80.lv Tips on stylized geometry coloring](https://80.lv/articles/tips-on-setting-stylized-geometry-coloring)
- **Hard silhouettes and flat normals:** a Blender Studio low-poly devlog uses hard low-poly silhouettes and simple flat normals — [Blender Studio devlog: Papercraft woods](https://studio.blender.org/blog/devlog-papercraft-woods/)
- **Colour-blind safety:** avoid green/red, green/brown and green/grey pairs where possible, and don't rely on colour alone for readability — [Smashing Magazine](https://smashingmagazine.com/2016/06/improving-ux-for-color-blind-users). Orange balls can be impossible to see against green grass for colour-blind people — [Colour Blind Awareness: sport](https://www.colourblindawareness.org/colour-blindness/living-with-colour-vision-deficiency/sport/). With achromatopsia, green grass and a grey street are barely distinguishable, so a disc-golf map used white dashed edge lines — [discgolf.com](https://discgolf.com/?p=95198). A golf-app user reported that tee-shot markers couldn't be told apart from the background — [Garmin forums](https://forums.garmin.com/apps-software/mobile-apps-web/f/garmin-connect-web/273553/golf-background-colors-bad-for-the-colorblind). Small contrast increases between foreground and background helped colour-blind viewers — [Colour Blind Awareness](https://www.colourblindawareness.org/?p=1648)
- **Colour depth on Godot Mobile:** the Mobile renderer uses RGB10A2 (medium dynamic range, low precision), while Forward+ uses RGBA16F — [Godot 4.7 renderers overview](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html). The Godot docs suggest debanding against the resulting banding — [search summary of Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)
- **Lighting on mobile:** realtime lighting, multiple shadows and GI are "especially expensive" and may be too much for "lower power mobile devices". Baked lighting is recommended for mobile. Keep the DirectionalLight3D Dynamic and set most omni and spot lights to Static. Static lights can't cast shadows onto baked meshes, which can make outdoor scenes look flat — [Godot 4.7 Optimizing 3D performance](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/tutorials/performance/optimizing_3d_performance.html)

### Inferences
- **Readability ladder (one value step per surface):** pick the values in grayscale first, then add hue. A possible order from darkest to lightest: water (dark, with a light rim or foam line) < trees and deep rough < rough < fairway < green (lightest grass) < sand (lightest overall). Keep rough and fairway at least one clear value step apart. Mowing stripes and a thin light outline at the fairway and green edges give a second cue that doesn't depend on colour, as the disc-golf map's white edge lines do.
- **The ball and markers** should be white with a dark outline or ring and a trail, never orange or red. Draw the cup flag and landing marker with a dark rim and a pattern, not hue alone. The aim and landing UI should keep the same colours in every theme.
- **Theme palettes:** keep a fixed "gameplay layer" (fairway, green, sand, water and UI values shared by every theme, so play reads the same) and give each theme its own "mood layer" (sky gradient, fog colour, far terrain, tree and prop tints, sun angle and colour). That keeps themes recognisable without hurting readability. The autumn and desert themes put red, orange and brown next to green, so check them with a CVD simulator and lean on value contrast.
- **Small-screen composition:** in portrait, the sky and backdrop take a big share of the frame in the behind-ball camera. A distinctive skyline silhouette (dunes, mesas, peaks, palms, volcano, city floodlights) does most of the "this is a different course" work, so spend art effort there rather than on ground detail you can barely see.
- **Time of day as a cheap variant:** move the directional light's angle and colour and shift the sky gradient and fog colour (dawn, midday, golden hour, night). One dynamic directional light with shadows near the ball only, plus blob shadows elsewhere (already planned), fits the Mobile renderer guidance.
- **Flat or gradient shading over PBR textures:** it reads better at phone size and costs almost no texture memory.

### Gaps
- I found no published colour-vision-deficiency guidance specific to golf games, and no measured contrast targets for terrain surfaces. The ladder above is my own synthesis of general CVD guidance.
- The one stylised-golf art article I tried to read failed (HTTP 403), so I have no first-hand golf-specific art-direction breakdown.

## 3. Cheap variety techniques

### Takeaway
Use one shared terrain shader that blends surfaces by a per-texel surface ID (the existing surface grid) with per-theme colour uniforms. Give all props and trees one palette texture (a single material). Use a theme-specific vegetation set drawn with MultiMesh, and a sky gradient plus fog per theme. Together these make most of the difference between themes for very little memory or authoring. A theme needs roughly 6-15 unique props.

### Cited Findings
- **One material via a palette atlas:** low-poly models use one material, with each face's UVs pointing at a colour cell in a shared palette texture. A Blender add-on plus Godot shader does exactly this with "a single material and a single draw call" — [Dynamic Color Palette (itch.io)](https://fwdotcom.itch.io/dynamic-color-palette). Two palettes on one asset means two materials and two draw calls, and merging them gets you back to one — [Low Poly Palette docs](https://superhivemarket.com/products/low-poly-palette/docs). "One of the more costly operations in rendering is the material change. A scene with a single material will perform much better than one with multiple materials" — [itch.io devlog: single material per scene](https://itch.io/devlog/73530/blazing-fast-rendering-with-a-single-material-per-scene.amp). Texture atlases and vertex colours are the main tools for packing variety into one material slot — [Tripo guide](https://www.tripo3d.ai/blog/explore/smart-mesh-minimizing-material-slots-in-low-poly-assets); [palette mesh optimisation](https://dmnshd.gg/blog/palette-mesh-optimization)
- **Arm's draw-call advice** also points to atlases: draw calls cost more when state changes (texture bindings, attribute bindings, enable settings), so atlas textures to cut state changes, and use offline static batching and instancing — [Arm community, Pete Harris](https://community.arm.com/support-forums/f/graphics-gaming-and-vr-forum/9238/draw-call-performance-on-the-mali-t880-mp12/30499)
- **MultiMesh** "allows the drawing of many thousands of objects at very little performance cost". **Automatic instancing is only in Forward+, not Mobile or Compatibility**, so on the Mobile renderer repeated objects must use MultiMesh explicitly. Alpha-blended materials are never auto-instanced — [Godot 4.7 Optimizing 3D performance](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/tutorials/performance/optimizing_3d_performance.html)
- **Decals and fog are supported** on the Mobile renderer, as are depth/height fog, glow, LightmapGI, MSAA, FXAA and tonemapping. **Not supported:** volumetric fog, SSAO, SSR, VoxelGI, SDFGI and directional-light PCSS. Light limits are 8 omni/spot lights per mesh (256 per view) and 8 reflection probes per mesh — [Godot 4.7 renderers overview](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)
- **Distance fade** on decals and lights is a LOD tool — [Godot 4.7 Optimizing 3D performance](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/tutorials/performance/optimizing_3d_performance.html)
- **CC0 asset sources** (free for commercial use, attribution optional):
  - Kenney: "all game assets on the asset pages are public domain licensed (CC0)… Attribution is not required… free to use them, even in commercial projects". Don't use the Kenney logo on its own — [Kenney support](https://www.kenney.nl/support). Kenney has a CC0 Minigolf Kit (125 files on kenney.nl; 60 objects on OpenGameArt) — [kenney.nl Minigolf Kit](https://www.kenney.nl/assets/minigolf-kit); [OpenGameArt](https://opengameart.org/content/minigolf-kit); [itch.io](https://kenney-assets.itch.io/minigolf-kit)
  - Quaternius: "All models are under the CC0 License", attribution "not necessary", usable "in commercial, educational, and personal projects", and modification permitted — [Quaternius FAQ](https://quaternius.com/faq.html). CC0 gives no warranty or indemnification — [licenseorg summary](https://licenseorg.com/guide/3d-assets/quaternius)
  - KayKit, Poly Haven and AmbientCG are also listed as CC0 sources by a non-authoritative aggregator, so verify each pack's own licence file — [Cinevva list](https://app.cinevva.com/game-assets/free-low-poly-3d-models)

### Inferences
- **Recipe for one theme (cheap):**
  1. **Terrain:** one ShaderMaterial for the whole course mesh, reading a small surface-ID texture baked from the existing surface grid (fairway, rough, green, sand, water edge, path, OB). Per-theme uniforms set the colour per surface, mowing-stripe strength, a noise tint for breakup, and edge-outline width. Swapping a theme means swapping one uniform set (a .tres resource), with no new textures.
  2. **Props and vegetation:** about 3 tree or plant species plus 3-6 rocks and props per theme, each about 100-600 triangles, all UV'd onto one shared 256-512 px palette atlas. A theme swap then just points at a different palette row. Draw each species as one MultiMeshInstance3D per hole chunk. Randomise per-instance colour (MultiMesh supports instance colour) and scale with the seeded Rng for variety.
  3. **Sky and atmosphere:** a gradient sky (ProceduralSkyMaterial or a small shader with horizon, zenith and sun-glow colours) plus depth fog in the theme's horizon colour. Fog also hides LOD pops and the course edge.
  4. **Backdrop:** one low-poly skyline ring mesh per theme (dunes, mesas, peaks, sea cliffs, a volcano), vertex coloured or on the palette atlas, so it costs a single draw call.
  5. **Decals** (sparingly): divots, a tee-box logo-free marker, leaf litter. Each has distance fade.
  6. **Variants:** night is the same course with a dark sky, an emissive-lit flag and markers, and a few Static omni "floodlights" baked into the palette tint. Autumn and winter are palette and vegetation swaps.
- **How many unique assets is enough:** a skyline silhouette, 2-3 vegetation species, about 3 rock/prop types and one hero landmark (lighthouse, windmill-style tower, cactus arch, cable-car pylon — all generic) make a theme recognisable at phone size. Beyond about 15 unique meshes per theme you mostly add memory and authoring time, not identity.
- **Mix CC0 packs carefully:** different packs have different proportions and palettes. Re-colour everything onto your own palette atlas so the courses look original and consistent, and record the source of every asset in ASSETS.md (the repo's asset-wrangler workflow).
- **Must stay original:** don't copy any commercial golf game's distinctive course look, colour scheme or landmark choices. Themes should come from real-world landscape types (above), not from game titles.

### Gaps
- I found no published "assets per theme" benchmarks from shipped games. The 6-15 range is my own estimate.
- I didn't verify KayKit's licence directly or check the current kenney.nl Minigolf Kit file count.
- I found no Godot-specific article benchmarking a single-shader, surface-ID terrain on mobile.

## 4. Mobile performance budgets for large open courses

### Takeaway
I found no authoritative 2025-2026 numbers for mid-range Mali or Adreno, and the draw-call guidance conflicts (Arm says about 500; general guides say 100-200 or "a few hundred"). The repo's own ≤100 draw calls is conservative and sensible for a 30 fps floor on low-end devices. Tile-based GPUs pay extra for geometry and overdraw, so cap visible triangles, avoid alpha-blended foliage, and use visibility ranges, fog and simple stand-ins for distant trees. Texture memory is small if you use palette atlases (a 1024² compressed texture with mipmaps is about 1.33 MiB).

### Cited Findings
- **Draw calls:** an Arm engineer suggests aiming for "~500 draw calls a frame for most mobile devices" and reducing them with static batching, instancing and atlases — [Arm community, Pete Harris](https://community.arm.com/support-forums/f/graphics-gaming-and-vr-forum/9238/draw-call-performance-on-the-mali-t880-mp12/30499). Unity's (older) mobile guide says "don't use more than a few hundred draw calls per frame on mobiles" — [Unity Manual: Mobile optimisation](https://docs.unity3d.com/2017.3/Documentation/Manual/MobileOptimisation.html). A less authoritative guide says under 100-200 — [kindatechnical](https://kindatechnical.com/game-development/performance-optimization-for-mobile-gpus.html). *These conflict.*
- **Vertices:** Unity's old guidance was about 40,000 visible vertices per frame on iPhone 3GS-class devices (very dated), with a per-mesh "sweet spot" of 1,500-4,000 triangles (desktop-oriented) — [Unity Manual: Optimizing graphics performance](https://docs.unity3d.com/352/Documentation/Manual/OptimizingGraphicsPerformance.html). Arm's Vulkan memory guidance is to keep vertex count below about 2 million (a memory limit, not a frame budget) — [Arm: memory limits with Vulkan on Mali](https://developer.arm.com/community/arm-community-blogs/b/mobile-graphics-and-gaming-blog/posts/memory-limits-with-vulkan-on-mali-gpus). Unity warns that one Android phone can be "10x more powerful for rendering" than another, so profile on target devices — [Unity Manual](https://docs.unity3d.com/2017.3/Documentation/Manual/MobileOptimisation.html)
- **Tile-based cost of geometry:** on tile-based GPUs, binning records only the tiles each triangle touches, so the vertex shader re-runs per triangle, per tile. Heavy geometry costs more than raw counts suggest — [Meta developer docs](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/unreal/gpu-impaired-algorithms/). Every tile has a small overhead, even for draws touching one tile — [Qualcomm Adreno FAQ](https://developer.qualcomm.com/sites/default/files/docs/adreno-gpu/snapdragon-game-toolkit/gdg/gpu/faq.html). Arm lists the main bottlenecks as too many draw calls, too many vertices and fragment processing, and recommends LOD for distant or small objects — [Arm mobile graphics optimisation deck (2018)](https://www.slideshare.net/OwenWu/mobile-graphics-optimization-guides); see also [Arm Vulkanised 2025 mobile best practices](https://www.vulkan.org/user/pages/09.events/vulkanised-2025/T18-Pete-Harris-Arm-MobileBestPractices.pdf)
- **Godot culling and LOD for big scenes:** without culling you may render "10× or 100× more than what is visible". Godot has visibility ranges (manual HLOD) and automatic mesh LOD on import, "most effective when used together". A single transparent quad can stand in for a distant tree, but stacked transparency eats fill rate. Grouping distant trees into one object works well for areas the player sees but can't reach. Impostors "can be complex to get working". Build big worlds in tiles. Use few transparent objects — [Godot 4.7 Optimizing 3D performance](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/tutorials/performance/optimizing_3d_performance.html)
- **Texture memory in Godot 4:** for an RGBA8 texture with mipmaps, VRAM-compressed is 21 KiB at 128² and 1.33 MiB at 1024², against 85 KiB and 5.33 MiB uncompressed (about 4:1). Mipmaps add roughly 33%. "High Quality" compression uses ASTC on mobile, and the default uses ETC2. Mobile usually can't display textures above 4096×4096 — [Godot 4.7 Importing images](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/tutorials/assets_pipeline/importing_images.html). ASTC ranges from 8 bpp down to under 1 bpp — [Arm GDC 2017 resources](https://community.arm.com/arm-community-blogs/b/mobile-graphics-and-gaming-blog/posts/gdc-17-developer-resources-released)
- **Mobile renderer limits:** 8 omni/spot lights per mesh, no SSAO/SSR/volumetric fog, LightmapGI supported — [Godot 4.7 renderers overview](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)

### Inferences
These are proposed budgets for Cup Dash, derived from the sources above plus the repo plan. Validate them on the test phone.

| Item | Proposed budget (per frame / per course) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Draw calls | ≤ 100 (plan), with a hard ceiling of about 150 | Well under Arm's ~500, leaving headroom for the 30 fps floor and the UI |
| Visible triangles | About 100k-150k at 60 fps on mid-range; about 60k on low-end (quality toggle) | No authoritative source; set by tile-GPU cost and profiling |
| Terrain | One mesh per hole chunk, about 10k-25k triangles, with LOD | Big flat areas need few triangles; spend them on green contours |
| Tree mesh | Near: 200-600 triangles, opaque or alpha-scissor (not alpha-blend). Far: a crossed-quad or 12-24 triangle low-poly cone/ball stand-in through visibility ranges | Godot's guidance on transparency and stand-ins |
| Trees per hole | A few hundred instances in 1-3 MultiMeshes; beyond about 150-250 m from the camera, fade into fog or merge into a "tree wall" mesh | MultiMesh is cheap; fill rate is the limit |
| Grass blades | None in 3D. Fake grass through the terrain shader (stripes, noise, edge outline) | Dense alpha grass is the main fill-rate risk on Mali/Adreno |
| Textures per course | About 2-6 MiB VRAM: palette atlas (256-512² ≈ 0.1-0.3 MiB), surface-ID map, small noise textures, sky gradient (no cubemap) | Godot's compressed figures above; a palette style needs few textures |
| Lights | One Dynamic DirectionalLight3D with shadows limited to a short distance around the ball; blob shadow for the ball; everything else Static or baked into the palette | Godot Mobile guidance |
| Post-processing | Tonemap, optional glow, debanding; no SSAO/SSR (unsupported on Mobile anyway) | Renderer overview |

- **How golf games keep 60 fps on big open courses:** the camera mostly looks down a corridor. Load and draw only the current hole's chunk, plus the backdrop ring and fog. Treat the next hole as a separate tile (Godot's "build the world in tiles" advice). The top-down green-read view sees few trees, so cull them by visibility range there.
- The flight camera covers large distances fast, so make sure LOD transitions are hidden by fog or happen beyond about 100 m. Use visibility-range fade margins (hysteresis) to avoid popping.
- Grouping static props per hole into one mesh with the palette atlas means one material and one draw call per group. Combined with MultiMesh trees, a hole could plausibly render in about 20-40 draw calls before UI.
