# Draw the demos live, skip the video

Pocket Arena 8 should not ship video files. It should replace the static 4-row icon panel with a short deck of **in-engine animated cards**: one gesture per card, each a 2.6-3.6 s loop drawn with `_draw` on a stylised mini-board in the game's own palette, with an original "fingertip" glyph whose timings follow Android's own touch constants. Then, during the first match, a few contextual ghost-hand hints do most of the real teaching. The evidence backs this split. In a 45,000-player experiment, up-front instruction barely helped simple games and could even cut return rates, while in-context teaching won wherever tutorials mattered at all ([Andersen et al. 2012](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/andersen2012tio.pdf)). In a separate trial, interactive help beat video, video came a close second, and text trailed ([Kao 2020](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dkao/pdf/kao2020fdg.pdf)). On the technical side, Godot 4.7 can play Ogg Theora on Android and can now record `.ogv` directly through Movie Maker. Even so, a live 2D demo costs **0 bytes of APK and close to 0 VRAM**. It stays crisp at every DPI, localises without re-rendering, and cannot fall out of step with the art. A clip costs about 0.5-1 MB, CPU decode every frame, and a re-record after every rules or art change. The concrete spec below has 3 cards for Serpent Grid, 5 for Stack Drop (the fifth only in vs mode), manual advance, auto-stop after 3 loops, tap-to-pause, a reduced-motion poster fallback that reuses today's icon drawings, and per-verb "learned" flags in SaveService. It needs no third-party assets and imitates no commercial game.

## Short, optional, in-context teaching beats any up-front tour

The strongest controlled evidence cuts against heavy tutorials for games like these. Andersen and colleagues A/B tested 8 tutorial conditions across 3 games with **over 45,000 players**. In the complex, unconventional game, context-sensitive tutorials produced **75% more levels and 29% longer play** than no tutorial. In the simple puzzle game, tutorials had no significant effect. In the platformer, context-sensitive tutorials left **3.5% fewer players returning** ([Andersen et al. 2012](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/andersen2012tio.pdf)). Blocking "do this now" steps helped in none of the three games, and an on-demand help button cut play time in the simple game by 15% ([Andersen et al. 2012](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/andersen2012tio.pdf)). That study predates touch-first mobile and tested text tutorials, not animation. Even so, its direction is clear: players learn more from exploring than from reading, and tutorials can hurt retention in ways nobody expects.

Format matters once some help is warranted. Kao randomly assigned 1,646 participants to six help conditions. **Interactive help ranked first and video "a close second"** on time spent, how learnable the controls felt, and motivation. Text and no help trailed, and offering every format at once ("All Help") did *not* beat one good format ([Kao 2020](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dkao/pdf/kao2020fdg.pdf)). App research points the same way. In NN/g's 70-person test, deck-of-cards tutorials did not improve task success (91% vs 94%) and made tasks feel *harder*, with perceived ease falling from 5.49 to 4.92 ([NN/g 2020](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-tutorials/)). NN/g still says instructions are expected in mobile games. It advises one concept per card, as few cards as possible, and a highly visible Skip ([NN/g onboarding](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-app-onboarding/)). It also warns that short-term memory "fades in about 20 seconds" ([NN/g coach marks](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-instructional-overlay/)).

Industry numbers are anecdotal but agree. One mobile title lost **60% of players during its tutorial** by moving between mechanics too fast, and up to a minute of unguided opening play also drove churn ([Supersonic 2025](https://supersonic.com/learn/blog/optimizing-ftue/)). A tutorial with no skip passed only **40%** of players, against a cited 70-80% norm, and drop-off clustered at the step 1→2 transition ([Game Developer 2017](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-to-optimize-the-tutorial-for-your-game)). No published dataset links skip rate to D1 retention for short-session arcade games, so any specific completion target is a hypothesis to test on the test phone.

Applied to these two games, the evidence points in two directions. Serpent Grid is simple and fits its genre, so heavy tutorials will not move retention. The one rule worth animating is **wrap-around edges**, because it contradicts the genre's expectation that walls kill. Stack Drop has five verbs, a non-obvious drag rule (one column per short step), a hidden two-finger shortcut for HOLD, and a relationship rule (cleared rows send garbage). That puts it between Andersen's simple and complex cases. Notably, the current Stack Drop panel never teaches the garbage rule; it appears only in the mode blurb. On timing, the sources support this order: show a short skippable deck on first launch, which the code already does through the `tutorial_seen` flag. Teach in context during the first match. Keep the deck replayable from the start screen's "How to play" button and from the pause menu, which the Game Accessibility Guidelines ask for ("allow all narrative and instructions to be replayed") ([GAG](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)). Do not add a persistent "?" badge during play, because of Andersen's help-button result.

The research notes disagree on auto-advance. One recommends auto-advance with a pause affordance. The other recommends manual advance, partly because WCAG 2.2.2 requires a pause, stop or hide control for motion that starts automatically, lasts more than 5 s and sits alongside other content ([W3C 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)). Manual advance wins here. It gives the player control, and it keeps a realistic deck to roughly 12 s for Serpent Grid and 20 s for Stack Drop if each card is watched for about one loop. Each demo also stops by itself after 3 loops, the same move as WCAG technique G152.

## Live 2D replays cost nothing; Theora costs about 0.5-1 MB per clip and CPU every frame

Godot 4.7 has exactly one core video codec. "The only supported format in core is Ogg Theora." WebM was **removed in 4.0**, and H.264 is excluded for patent reasons. Decoding runs on the CPU, and the docs advise **at most 720p and preferably 30 fps or lower on mobile** ([Godot 4.7: Playing videos](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/animation/playing_videos.html)). Looping needs Loop on and Video Delay Compensation set to 0. The page documents no alpha channel, and its only transparency route is a chroma-key shader ([Godot 4.7](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/animation/playing_videos.html)). The well-known 4.3 frame-timing bug was fixed for **4.4** ([godot#101837](https://github.com/godotengine/godot/issues/101837)), so the project's 4.7.2 is clear of it. No primary-source Android decode benchmark exists for Theora on 4.4-4.7. A GodotFest 2025 talk still calls the codec outdated, with no hardware decode and no zero-copy path ([media.ccc.de](https://app.media.ccc.de/v/godotfest2025-from-theora-to-av1-transforming-video-workflows-in-godot-4)).

Producing clips would be easy in this repo. Movie Maker renders at a fixed fps that is not tied to real time, and since 4.5 it can write **OGV directly**. Recording needs an editor build ([Godot 4.7: Creating movies](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/animation/creating_movies.html)). A local run on 2026-11-13 (Godot 4.7.2, commit 08da76d) used the existing `--game --bot --autostart --seed` flags. It produced 12 s, 30 fps clips at 432x864 (the project's window override clamps `--resolution`), with audio: **Stack Drop 1.17 MB (~0.78 Mbit/s) and Serpent Grid 2.04 MB (~1.36 Mbit/s)**. Recording took about 5.4 s of wall time, and the full HUD and countdown were captured. Scaling those bitrates gives roughly **0.45-0.8 MB per 6 s 540x540 clip**, and 0.6-1.1 MB at 720x720. Because `.ogv` is already compressed, the APK zip layer will not shrink it further. That is affordable, but it buys little. The clip would carry the HUD unless a dedicated demo scene is built. Any caption or finger sprite burned into the video multiplies clip count by language. Every gravity, board-size or palette change silently makes a clip stale. The last five commits on this branch changed gravity, board size twice, and CPU tuning.

Sprite sheets and Lottie lose more clearly. `AnimatedTexture` is **deprecated** in 4.7: it "does not work properly in current versions" ([Godot 4.7: AnimatedTexture](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_animatedtexture.html)). The Lottie importer PR is still open, bakes frames to SpriteFrames, and is editor-only ([godot#91580](https://github.com/godotengine/godot/pull/91580)). A community runtime reportedly fails on Android ([Godot forum](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/how-to-support-lottie-animations-in-godot/129644)). Full frames at 540x540 RGBA come to about 1.17 MB each. A 6 s, 30 fps sheet would need about 210 MB uncompressed, or about 26 MB even with ETC2. FFmpeg or AV1 GDExtensions bring tens of MB of native libraries; the GoZen download alone is 148 MB ([itch.io](https://voylin.itch.io/gde-gozen-video-playback-addon-for-godot)).

Live demos exploit what the project already has. The rules are pure and deterministic, and the demo is not simulation, so it can run off Tweens at render rate. A 3D `SubViewport` can host the real game view. Its defaults include `UPDATE_WHEN_VISIBLE`, and `UPDATE_WHEN_PARENT_VISIBLE` is available ([Godot 4.7: SubViewport](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_subviewport.html)). The catch is cost: it adds a second Mobile-renderer pass on top of the paused game drawn underneath, at about 1.2 MB of colour target at 540x540 plus depth, and nobody has published Adreno numbers for it. A stylised 2D `_draw` mini-board avoids all of that. It slots into the existing architecture, where `GameShell._build_howto()` already calls a per-step draw `Callable` into a `Control`.

| Route | APK add | Runtime memory | Per-frame cost | Stays correct after rule/art changes | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stylised 2D `_draw` mini-board (recommended) | 0 | ~0 | canvas draw calls only | yes (draws from palette; scripted states tested) | Ship |
| 3D SubViewport replay of real view | 0 | ~1.2 MB colour + depth at 540² | extra GPU pass, unprofiled on Adreno 642L | yes | Optional hero card after profiling |
| Theora `.ogv` 540², 6 s, no audio | ~0.45-0.8 MB/clip (from measured bitrates) | ~1-2 MB | CPU decode + YUV→RGB each frame | no, must re-record | Use only for Play Store promo video |
| Sprite sheet | ~10-26 MB/clip (ETC2) | same, resident | trivial | no | Reject |
| Lottie / FFmpeg GDExtension | n/a / tens of MB | — | — | — | Reject |

The Movie Maker pipeline is still worth keeping, just not inside the APK. It is the right tool for the store listing's promo video and for review GIFs of the new deck. Any scripted replay, live or recorded, carries one determinism risk. A tick-stamped input list replays exactly only while gravity, board size, kick tables and RNG consumption stay unchanged. So store demos as *intents*: target placements for Stack Drop, turn points for Serpent Grid. Guard each with a golden-replay gdUnit test that fails whenever a `RULES_VERSION` constant changes.

## Dots, rings and trails borrow Android's own clock

No study compares a hand glyph with an abstract touch dot. The most cited practitioner notation uses both: a simplified line-drawn hand as "the key orienting element", drawn opaque so it visibly occupies space, plus a plain dot for a touch, a ringed dot for press-and-hold, and arrows for direction ([UXmatters, Hoober 2019](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2019/05/a-design-language-for-touch-gesture-and-motion.php)). Material guidance adds two points: animate elements to suggest a gesture, and during a gesture move things in a way that shows its purpose ([Material: Gestures](https://m2.material.io/design/interaction/gestures.html)). Apple notes that gestures "are invisible" and recommends animation to reveal them ([WWDC21](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2021/10126/)). Concretely, the world must react on the same beat as the finger: the snake turns the instant the swipe ends.

Android supplies defensible numbers for each phase. The Compose ripple fades in over **75 ms**, expands its radius over **225 ms** from 0.3x, and fades out over **150 ms** ([AOSP RippleAnimation.kt](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/support/+/androidx-main/compose/material/material-ripple/src/commonMain/kotlin/androidx/compose/material/ripple/RippleAnimation.kt)). `ViewConfiguration` defaults to a **100 ms tap timeout, 400 ms long-press, 300 ms double-tap window, 8 dp touch slop and 50 dp/s minimum fling velocity** ([AOSP ViewConfiguration](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/main/core/java/android/view/ViewConfiguration.java)). For motion, Material 3 sets duration tokens from 50 to 1000 ms, with standard easing `cubic-bezier(0.2, 0, 0, 1)`, emphasized decelerate `(0.05, 0.7, 0.1, 1)` for entrances and accelerate curves for exits ([Material Components: Motion](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Motion.md)). NN/g puts most UI moves at **100-500 ms** and calls linear motion unnatural ([NN/g: Animation duration](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)). Android also caps blink speed at no faster than one per **333 ms** for photosensitivity ([Android ViewConfiguration](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/view/ViewConfiguration)), which sets a floor for any "tap here" pulse.

The design rule that follows is that **speed and shape, not colour, separate the gestures**. A tap is a press, a ripple and a lift. A swipe is a 200 ms ease-out stroke with a tapered trail. A drag is slow and stepped: the finger dwells about 100 ms at each column while the piece snaps one column per step, with column tick marks and no streak. That last part matters because a demo that hides the stepping will make players think drag is broken. A flick is a 120 ms ease-in burst with speed lines and an immediate impact flash, 3-5x faster than one drag step. A 120 ms, 80 dp flick works out to about 670 dp/s, well inside Android's fling range. A two-finger tap lands two dots within 40 ms of each other and adds an "x2" badge, so the count does not depend on spotting two dots. A loop should pace its slowness through holds and pauses, not slow easing. The pattern is enter, anticipate, act, react, hold the result, exit, then rest. These sequence durations are a synthesis of the Material and NN/g ranges, not a published standard. No source specifies the pause between loop repeats or how many repeats to show.

## Cards, dots and fallbacks must work silent, small and in German

NN/g's card guidance is strict: one focused interaction per hint, text "as short as possible", visuals paired with very short text, and a visual style distinct from live UI so users do not try to tap the hint ([NN/g coach marks](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-instructional-overlay/)). Its 2023 piece calls tutorials "dramatically overused" and "quickly forgotten". It recommends help that can be dismissed and reached again later, and hands-on tutorials that can be revisited ([NN/g onboarding tutorials](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/onboarding-tutorials/)). There is no authoritative number for caption word count, demo aspect ratio or dot size, so the numbers in the next section are design judgment checked against platform floors.

Those floors are firm. Touch targets must be at least **48 x 48 dp** (about 123 px on this canvas), and text below 18 pt needs at least **4.5:1** contrast ([Android accessibility](https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/ui/accessibility/apps)). Graphical objects needed to understand content need 3:1 ([W3C 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). Colour cannot be the only carrier ([W3C 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)), and nothing may flash more than 3 times per second ([W3C 2.3.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)). Instructions must not rely on text alone or on sound alone ([GAG](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)). That fits muted mobile play: the animation carries the meaning, the caption backs it up, and sound adds nothing essential. For reduced motion, Android's "Remove animations" toggle sets `ANIMATOR_DURATION_SCALE` to 0 ([AOSP Settings](https://git.replicant.us/replicant-next/packages_apps_Settings/plain/src/com/android/settings/accessibility/DisableAnimationsPreferenceController.java)). Apps read it through `Settings.Global` and default to 1 if the setting is missing ([Panula, dev.to](https://dev.to/eevajonnapanula/android-animations-and-reduced-motion-2em2)). Godot exposes no API for this, so v1 should add an in-game "Reduce motion" toggle, which is a WCAG 2.3.3 technique in its own right ([W3C 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)). Reading the OS value can follow later through a small Android plugin. In that mode each card shows a static poster. Today's `_draw_howto` and `_icon_*` functions already are those posters, so the current panel becomes the accessible fallback instead of dead code.

Localisation favours the live route. Text stays in labels, never in a texture or a clip. Captions are verb-first and one line, and the layout leaves about 35% horizontal slack for longer languages; that slack figure is a common rule of thumb, not sourced here. Before shipping, run the project's ip-checker agent over the new strings.

## The recommended design for Serpent Grid and Stack Drop

Everything below is a concrete synthesis. The cited ranges come from the sections above. The exact values are proposals to confirm with a screenshot pass and a session on the test phone.

**Flow.** First launch of each game auto-opens the deck, keeping the existing `tutorial_seen` behaviour. A Skip button is visible from frame 0, and the last card's primary button reads **PLAY** and starts the match directly, so nobody returns to the start screen just to press Play again. "How to play" stays on the start screen and is also added to the pause menu. Cards advance manually through Next, Back and the progress dots. Horizontal swipes do *not* page the deck, because players will copy the gestures inside the demo and a mimicked swipe must not skip a card. Each demo loops 3 times, then freezes on its result frame with a 123 px replay chip. Tapping the demo stage pauses or resumes it and shows a pause glyph (WCAG G4). Mode filtering drops the CPU-only cards in Solo.

**Cards.**

| Game | # | Title (≤4 words) | Sub-line (≤8 words) | Demo content | Loop |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Serpent Grid | 1 | Swipe to turn | Anywhere on screen. Orbs make you grow. | Finger swipes in empty space away from the snake; snake turns on lift, eats an orb, grows one segment | 3.0 s |
| Serpent Grid | 2 | Edges wrap around | Leave one side, return on the other. | Head exits the right edge; both edges glow for 300 ms; head re-enters on the left | 3.2 s |
| Serpent Grid | 3 (vs) | Make the CPU crash | Cut it off with your body. | Player snake swings across the CPU snake's path; CPU head hits the body; crash burst + "+1" | 3.6 s |
| Stack Drop | 1 | Tap to rotate | Shards slide off walls when needed. | Tap ripple anywhere; shard rotates 90° twice per loop | 2.6 s |
| Stack Drop | 2 | Drag to move | Each short step is one column. | Finger steps 3 columns right with tick marks; shard snaps per step; landing ghost follows | 3.2 s |
| Stack Drop | 3 | Flick down to drop | A quick flick slams it home. | Fast downward flick with speed lines; shard drops; impact flash | 2.6 s |
| Stack Drop | 4 | Tap HOLD to keep | Or tap with two fingers. | Finger taps the HOLD chip; shard swaps into it; then two dots with "x2" badge repeat the action | 3.4 s |
| Stack Drop | 5 (vs) | Clear rows, send garbage | Overflow the CPU's tower to win. | Row clears; 3 sparks arc to a mini CPU well; one grey row rises there | 3.6 s |

**Layout (1080-wide canvas, centred vertically inside the safe area).** The card panel is 952 px wide with 64 px side margins and the existing `UiKit` overlay style. The top row holds the game accent title "HOW TO PLAY" at 48 px on the left and a Skip text button on the right, padded to a 123 px tall target. The demo stage is a **920 x 920** rounded panel (radius 36) with a subtle inner vignette and a small "DEMO" tag, which keeps it visibly separate from live UI. Serpent Grid's stage holds a 9x9 grid of 96 px cells. Stack Drop's holds a 6-wide x 9-tall mini-well of 96 px cells, with HOLD and mini-CPU chips at the side. A 96 px column is about 37 dp, which matches the drag step size. Below the stage sit the caption title at **52 px, weight 800, white** and the sub-line at **34 px, `UiKit.MUTED`**, which must be checked for 4.5:1 contrast on the card background. Under those are the progress dots: 16 px circles with 20 px gaps, the active one a filled 44 x 16 pill and the inactive ones 3 px outlines, so state is shown by shape as well as colour. The bottom row holds a ghost Back button on the left and a green Next/PLAY button on the right, both **130 px tall** like the existing buttons, giving a combined footprint of about 1,450 px.

**Glyph.** The glyph is an original rounded "fingertip capsule", 84 x 150 px, white at 88% alpha with a 6 px dark outline so it holds 3:1 contrast on both light and dark cells. It drops a soft shadow offset 8 px down. The contact dot is a 28 px solid core in white or yellow, with neon glow as decoration only, since glow does not count toward contrast. The ripple ring expands from 37 px to 123 px diameter with a 6 px stroke. Trails taper from 14 px at the head to 0 and cover 50% of the stroke length. On Stack Drop the glyph enters from below-right, which reads as a right thumb. The glyph is hidden on tiny stages under about 500 px, where only the dot and trail are drawn. The design takes nothing from stock gesture packs or any product's hand art.

**Timeline per loop (ms; easings from Material 3).**

| Phase | Tap | Swipe | Drag (3 cols) | Flick | Two-finger tap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Enter (fade + 60 px slide, emphasized decelerate) | 250 | 250 | 250 | 250 | 250 |
| Anticipate (scale 1.0→0.95) | 120 | 120 | 120 | 120 | 120 |
| Action | contact 100; ripple 75 in / 225 grow / 150 out | 200 ease-out over 240 px; chevron at head | 3 x (60 move + 100 dwell) = 480 over 288 px | 120 ease-in over 200 px; 3 ghost copies | dots 40 ms apart, each with ripple |
| World reacts (same beat) | rotate 150 | turn on lift, orb eaten 250 | snap per step | drop 120 + flash 100 | swap 200 |
| Hold result | 700 | 700 | 600 | 700 | 700 |
| Exit (accelerate) | 200 | 200 | 200 | 200 | 200 |
| Rest before repeat | 800 | 800 | 800 | 800 | 800 |

Calm pulses, such as the HOLD chip highlight and the edge glow, use a period of at least 900 ms. Impact flashes stay below 3 per second and never fill the frame in saturated red.

**Architecture.** A new `shared/ui/howto_deck.gd` replaces `_build_howto()`. The `howto` config entry becomes a list of `{demo, poster, title, sub, modes}`, and `poster` is the existing icon callable. Gesture glyph poses come from a pure, Node-free `shared/howto/gesture_timeline.gd`: given a phase table and `t`, it returns position, scale, alpha and ripple radius. That makes the timing table above unit-testable. Each game supplies a demo script under `games/<id>/logic/` as intents (target placements, turn points) plus a seed for the project `Rng`. Check whether `StackLogic` and `SnakeLogic` accept a board size smaller than 12x24 or the full grid. If they do, drive the real logic on the mini-board. If not, use keyframed states, and still pin them with a golden test. The deck sets `physics_interpolation_mode = OFF` on its root, as CLAUDE.md requires for anything moved in `_process`. It preallocates its trail `PackedVector2Array` so it adds no per-frame allocations. Its timelines stop when the deck is hidden.

**First-match hints (phase 2).** These are non-blocking and never freeze the game:

- **Serpent Grid:** show a ghost-swipe if the player has not swiped within 4 s of GO. Pulse both edges the first time the head comes within 3 cells of an edge.
- **Stack Drop:** show a tap hint if nothing has rotated by the third shard. Show a flick hint if nothing has hard-dropped by the sixth. Play a one-time garbage arc to the CPU well on the first clear in vs mode. Show a "two-finger tap also holds" toast after the first press of the HOLD button.

Each hint retires for good once its verb has been performed. Store the flags as `verbs_done` in `SaveService.games[<id>]`, with a schema bump and `migrate()`. Add local counters for deck shown, skipped and completed, which can answer "did they learn it?" on the test phone without telemetry.

**"Try it" (phase 3).** The strongest format in Kao's data is interactive. After the loop has played once, the demo stage itself can accept the real gesture through the game's existing input code. The glyph fades, the mini-board responds, a tick appears, and Next pulses. This copies the pattern of Android's gesture-navigation tutorial, which has the user perform each gesture in a sandbox that recognises it ([Launcher3](https://gerrit.omnirom.org/plugins/gitiles/android_packages_apps_Launcher3/+/b55d5b279b3902e57f079afb628d10607bd42ac5%5E1..b55d5b279b3902e57f079afb628d10607bd42ac5)).

**Done criteria.** Each phase must pass these checks:

- **Tests:** gdUnit coverage for timeline totals, the 3-loop stop, Solo filtering, Skip/PLAY signals, and golden demo replays.
- **Screenshots:** one per card, taken at the hold-result timestamp through a debug flag.
- **Profiling:** a frame-time check with `--fps` on the test phone.
- **Docs:** an entry in `docs/PROGRESS.md`.
- **Plan:** the work touches more than 3 files, so start it with a plan in `docs/plans/`.

## Conclusion

The request for "beautiful how-to videos" is best met by something that only looks like a video. Because the games' logic is pure, seeded and fixed-step, the engine can draw its own tutorials more cheaply and more accurately than any encoded clip. The same choice also produces the accessible fallback (today's icons), the localisation path (text kept in labels) and a regression guard (golden replays that fail when the rules change). The bigger lesson from the evidence is that the deck is the least important layer. For a simple game like Serpent Grid, almost all the learning happens in the first 30 seconds of play. Effort is therefore better spent on hints that teach wrap-around edges and Stack Drop's stepped drag at the moment they matter than on polishing the cards.

Three things are still unknown and should be measured on the test phone before scaling this design to the other six games: real frame cost on the Adreno 642L, whether players try to mimic gestures inside the demo stage, and how often anyone replays the deck. Until then, every duration and size in the spec is a defensible starting point rather than a validated optimum.
