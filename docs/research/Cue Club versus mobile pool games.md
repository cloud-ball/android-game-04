# Cue Club needs livelier breaks before shipping

Cue Club matches or beats well-reviewed mobile pool games on controls, rules and technical soundness, but it is not ready for public release yet. Three things block it: breaks that come up dry at the power casual players actually use, a CPU whose difficulty has been tuned by feel but never measured, and audio that misses the cues players praise most. The controls follow the genre's mainstream: coarse aim, a separate fine-aim strip, a separate pull-down power bar, and a spin panel. They also already guard against the most-repeated control complaint in store reviews, aim shifting on finger lift or during the power stroke. The rules engine, the deterministic solver (bit-identical on Android at 144 µs per substep) and the offline, ad-free, sign-in-free design avoid most other 1-star themes. The gaps sit in the moments players judge first and most often. Measured headless, our break pockets a ball on only **3–25% of breaks at 8–10 m/s**, where pros on 7-ft tables manage about **80%**. Because the break uses a 12·p² power curve, 9 m/s is what a player gets at **87% of the bar**. The CPU shoots straight-ins only and plays no position, so cue-ball leaves are random, and players read random good leaves as skill. Cushions make no sound and every click plays at the same volume. Feature depth (no practice table, one rule set, a single rack) is thin next to standalone apps, but that matters less for one game in an eight-game hub than for a dedicated pool app. The fix list is short. About two weeks of focused work would close the blockers and the highest-value polish without copying any commercial game. Cue Club does not have to ship alongside Serpent Grid, so it can come later as an update once these are done.

## Controls and rules already sit at the genre's best practice

The survey of well-rated offline pool apps and press reviews shows a stable control pattern. Players drag to rotate the cue, use a separate fine-tune control, set power with a separate bar or pull-back, and pick spin on a cue-ball inset ([iMore tips](https://www.imore.com/8-ball-pool-6-tips-and-hints-beginners); [Arkadium help centre](https://support.arkadium.com/en/support/solutions/articles/44002181779--8-ball-pool-how-to-play-settings-daily-challenge-multiplayer-tips-scoring)). Cue Club's control set matches that pattern. More importantly, it already defends against the complaint that recurs across seven years of reviews: the aim moving as the finger lifts or as the power stroke starts. An arcade pool review says "the aim trajectory can move slightly after you lift your finger" ([iMore review, 2018](https://imore.com/pocket-run-pool-review-arcade-pool-makes-comeback)). Offline 8-ball users wrote "when I'm pulling the stick back on the power button it moves your shot" (2023) and "when you try to shoot, the aim screws up" (2025) ([8 Ball Billiards - Offline, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/8-ball-billiards-offline/id1559177737)). `PoolAim` reverts the aim to where it was **60 ms before release**, and power lives on its own strip, so pulling power cannot touch the aim. The pull-down bar is also safer than flick-to-shoot, which reviewers report misfiring and clashing with gesture navigation ([Pocket Run Pool, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id949982914)). The fixed, near-top-down portrait camera matches what players use for aiming on small screens. Even 3D-app reviewers say they mostly play top-down ([Real Pool 3D, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/real-pool-3d/id1156081906)), and handheld reviews fault behind-the-cue cameras that hide the table ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/reviews/switch-eshop/pure_pool)).

Point-to-aim does have one real flaw, and it comes from a departure from the earlier design research. That research proposed an Easy-only snap of about 0.5° to the nearest legal ball's *ball-to-pocket* line. The shipped snap locks to the **ball's centre** within 0.6° on Easy and 0.3° on Medium (`pool_game.gd` `_point`). By our own small-angle calculation, a 0.6° snap at 1 m is a 10.5 mm contact offset, or a **cut of about 10.6°**; at 0.5 m it is about 5°. On Easy, then, pointing alone cannot aim a gentle cut, which is the most common shot in a rack. A 10° error on a ball 0.5 m from the pocket misses by roughly 9 cm. The player has to discover the FINE strip, which beginners are the least likely to use, and any later point-drag snaps the aim back. "Aim snaps to angles instead of allowing infinite freedom" already appears as a complaint about an Android pool app ([Pool Billiards Pro, Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.forthblue.pool&hl=en_US)). The fix is to snap to ghost-ball aim lines for makeable (ball, pocket) pairs, which `PoolCpu._candidate` already computes, to switch snap off on the break, and to offer it as a setting.

The guideline sits at the generous end of the genre range. The Easy object-ball ray is 2.0 m, a full table length, which answers the "make the line longer" requests on Android ([Pool Billiards offline, Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.andregal.android.poolbilliard&hl=en_GB)). The ray comes from the solver itself, so it includes throw. Two gaps remain against simulation apps. Assist level is tied to CPU difficulty, whereas one long-running app offers "5 handicap levels with aiming and ball-guide mark-ups" separately from its 25 CPU opponents ([Pro Pool 2026, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/au/app/pro-pool-2024/id556429076)). There is also no guide-off option, which console reviewers credit with making players better ([MoviesGamesAndTech, 2026](https://moviesgamesandtech.com/2026/02/12/review-pure-pool-pro)). Since Cue Club has no online play, nothing argues for short lines or for paying coins to lengthen them.

The Casual 8 rules (call only the 8, ball in hand anywhere after every foul, re-spot an 8 potted on the break, "group cleared" snapshotted at shot start) sit at the forgiving end of the casual conventions ([Arkadium](https://support.arkadium.com/en/support/solutions/articles/44002181779--8-ball-pool-how-to-play-settings-daily-challenge-multiplayer-tips-scoring)), and 40 fixture tests cover them. Foul banners give the reason, which counters the "AI stealing an extra shot" perception ([8 Ball Billiards - Offline](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/8-ball-billiards-offline/id1559177737)). There is no shot clock against the CPU, which avoids another named complaint from the same listing.

## The first shot of every rack is the weakest physics

The solver's inputs are sound. Sliding 0.2, rolling 0.012, spin decay 10.9 rad/s², ball COR 0.93, ball friction 0.055 and cushion COR 0.85 all sit inside Dr. Dave's published ranges ([Dr. Dave: physical properties](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/?p=2349)). The cushion contact height is exactly WPA's 63.5% nose height ([WPA specs](https://www.poolroom.com/tournament-table-equipment-specifications/)). Headless measurements at commit b66a1be confirm the shapes players feel without naming them. A rolling cue ball deflects about 30° across the 14–49° cut range, peaking at 30.1° (ideal 33.7°), and stun cuts split at 80–86° ([Dr. Dave: 30-degree rule](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/FAQ/30-90-rules/30-degree-rule/); [how conditions affect the rules](https://www.engr.colostate.edu/~dga/pool/30-90-rules/conditions/)). Corner pockets accept far wider bands at slow speed and shallow angles (110–125 mm against a 61 mm geometric gap), and the side pocket's window shrinks from 84 mm head-on to 20 mm at 60°, as Dr. Dave describes qualitatively ([Dr. Dave: pocket size and centre](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/FAQ/pocket/size-and-center/)). Cushions are not "mirror" reflections, the classic fake-physics tell ([tailuge: why most pool games feel wrong](https://billiards.tailuge.workers.dev/blog1.html)). Full running english widens a 30° rebound by about 12°.

The break is where real players will notice something wrong. Top pros break at **11–13 m/s** ([Dr. Dave: break speeds](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/?p=1895)). On 7-ft bar tables they made a ball on about **80%** of breaks in Dr. Dave's 169-game sample, with a **2–7.6%** scratch rate ([Billiards Digest, Aug 2016](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/bd_articles/2016/aug16.pdf)). Across major events the made-ball rate ranges from 37% to 78% ([Dr. Dave: break statistics](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/faq/break/stats/)). Over 60 seeded racks per speed, our solver made a ball on **2–3% of breaks at 6–8 m/s, 3–18% at 9–10 m/s, 40% at 11 m/s and 53% at 12 m/s**, with **15% scratches** on a full-power stun break. Because of the 12·p² break curve, a player at 87% of the bar breaks at about 9 m/s. Only a maxed bar reaches the 12 m/s band, and that is also where a stun break scratches one time in seven. The CPU breaks at a fixed 11 m/s, so it pockets on about 40% of breaks. One diagnostic stands out. With zero rack jitter, 80 breaks pocketed nothing, and adding jitter (0.5 mm) did not help. The dry breaks therefore come from how energy splits through a near-symmetric rigid rack, not from the friction constants. A full-power break with slight draw already gives 75% made and 1.7% scratches. Snapping the human's break aim to the apex ball's centre, which makes the hit perfectly head-on, may make breaks deader still. That last point is our hypothesis and has not been measured. Amateur break data does not exist in the sources, so the proposed 9–10 m/s target (made ball on at least 40% of breaks) is a design target, not a literature value.

The rails are slightly dead. The WPA rail test asks for 4–4.5 table lengths from a firm centre-ball stroke ([WPA specs](https://www.poolroom.com/tournament-table-equipment-specifications/)). At 4.5 m/s, the top of Dr. Dave's "power shot" band ([Dr. Dave: typical speeds](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/faq/speed/typical/)), Cue Club manages **3.36 lengths**, and 4.0 needs about 6 m/s. A rolling ball driven straight into a rail comes back at 55% of its speed once it rolls again. Raising cushion COR to about 0.95 (Mathavan measured 0.98 in a different model, [Mathavan et al. 2010](https://dspace.lboro.ac.uk/2134/15087)), alone or with rolling friction 0.010, reaches 3.9–4.1 lengths without leaving the published ranges. "Balls … roll unnatural distances at unconvincing speeds" is exactly how reviewers describe weaker pool games ([PocketGamer: Virtual Pool HD](https://www.pocketgamer.com/virtual-pool-hd/review/)).

Three subtler errors matter mostly to experienced players. First, object-ball throw is a constant **3.15° for every cut of about 20° or more, at any speed or roll**. Alciatore's model gives about 4.7° for a slow stun cut, 1.7° for a fast one, and about 1.0° for a medium rolling hit ([Dr. Dave TP A.14](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/technical_proofs/new/TP_A-14.pdf)). Most game shots arrive rolling, so object balls run about 2° thick, which is bigger than a corner pocket's ±1.4° tolerance from 1.5 m. The solver-drawn guideline hides this on Easy and Medium, but it teaches a habit that will not carry over to a real table. Second, cushion angles and ratios do not change with speed, because Han's impulse is linear in velocity. Reverse english narrows a 30° rebound by only about 2°, against 12° of widening for running english. Third, fast balls lose only 5% of pocket window (a real table punishes slamming more), which experienced players will read as "slamming works". None of these three blocks shipping. Casual audiences complain about spin that "does nothing" ([Real Pool 3D, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/real-pool-3d/id1156081906)) far more than about throw curves. Each change also moves the golden break, so `.claude/rules/cue-sim.md` requires an updated golden file with a written justification.

## A straight-in-only CPU invites the lucky-leave complaint

Raw accuracy is not what players most resent in pool CPUs. They resent behaviour that gives the machine away. Mick West, who shipped a snooker AI, found that a client still called Easy "too good" after its aim was made worse. The computer "always seemed to be leaving the white ball in a convenient position", even though the cue ball's rest was never computed, and his lesson was that "random outcomes that happen to favor the computer are perceived as being intentional" ([West, Game Developer 2009](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/intelligent-mistakes-how-to-incorporate-stupidity-into-your-ai-code)). Cue Club's CPU has exactly this profile. It takes the clearest straight-in, computes no position, and picks at random among its best 4 shots (Easy) or best 2 (Medium). When it has ball in hand it always sets up a straight shot. That is the likeliest reason the user found it too strong even after the noise was doubled. If the analytic tolerance formula holds, the current σ values are already very weak: Easy at σ 1.4° pots only about **38% of short straight-ins and 9% of medium 30° cuts**. Physics balls are regulation size (0.028575 m), so the on-screen enlargement does not widen tolerances. The likelier causes are lucky leaves, straight shots after every ball-in-hand, and a human who also misses a lot. Lowering accuracy further would make misses look random and robotic and would lengthen racks. AI Factory, which shipped pool AI, warns of the opposite failure as well: an engine that "still chose difficult shots and then missed them" looks incomprehensible ([AI Factory, 2008](https://aifactory.co.uk/newsletter/2008_01_pool_tutor.htm)). Picking uniformly among the top 4 can produce that look.

The evidence points to a "mistake menu" rather than more noise. Keep σ moderate and draw plausible errors: thin or thick misses on hard cuts, under-hits that leave a ball in the jaws, pots that leave poor position, and on Easy a slightly harder ball than the obvious one. Include West-style misses that leave the human a makeable shot, and never an exotic shot. Vary the outcomes on purpose. Snooker 19 players called misses that left them safe "99 percent of the time", always in corner pockets, "so scripted" ([Steam: Snooker 19](https://steamcommunity.com/app/887800/discussions/0/1678064284160398975)). Reject Easy/Medium candidates whose noiseless miss would fluke a ball, and never lose deliberately on the 8. Visible rubber-banding is resented: a Virtual Pool 4 player saw the AI "play impossible shots multiple times if they are behind" ([Steam: Virtual Pool 4](https://steamcommunity.com/app/336150/discussions/0/1742227264189864867)). If adjustment is used at all, it should change only mistake frequency, stay hidden and small, and be off on Hard ([Compton, Game Developer 2019](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/more-than-meets-the-eye-the-secrets-of-dynamic-difficulty-adjustment)). Suggesting a level change after three straight results is more honest.

The current gaps are acceptable on Easy, where a beginner who "has a go" with no safe option is human-like, but they cap Hard. Mobile reviewers resent a top level that "never misses a double or a plant" and a CPU that "leaves you set up" after a safety ([Pro Pool 2026, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/au/app/pro-pool-2024/id556429076)). One reviewer praised a top level that "almost never misses, but also rarely runs more than a couple balls before needing to play defensive" ([Pro Snooker & Pool, appshunter](https://appshunter.io/ios/app/1586517737)). Hard therefore needs position play and occasional safeties more than it needs accuracy. The computational-pool literature says breaks matter too: CueCard's precomputed, keep-the-turn break accounted for about 10% of its wins ([Archibald et al., IJCAI 2009](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/09/Papers/231.pdf)). The current fixed 11 m/s apex break should become 2–3 precomputed breaks per level.

There are reassurances too. Think time of about 0.9 s plus 0.8 s of visible aim sits inside the 1.2–2.5 s band that avoids the "bot took forever" complaint ([itch.io](https://itch.io/post/6274367)). Cutting & Deterding found that whether players won, drew or lost had no significant effect on enjoyment ([Royal Society Open Science 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9890114/)), so a generous Easy carries little risk. What matters is that the CPU feels beatable and that the player can see progress. A 2026 meta-analysis reports a medium-to-large enjoyment penalty for computer opponents in general ([Ito, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24749); the ID looks inconsistent with its stated date, so verify it before quoting). That is one argument for adding cheap pass-and-play.

## Sound, not graphics, is the biggest presentation gap

Across pool reviews, contact audio draws more praise than any other single presentation element: "the best use of audio is the sound of the balls as they hit each other, thud into the cushions and fall into the pockets" ([Entertainment Focus](https://entertainment-focus.com/?p=61059)). Reviewers also call cloth-roll sound "a joy to listen to" ([MoviesGamesAndTech, 2026](https://moviesgamesandtech.com/2026/02/12/review-pure-pool-pro)). A low-rated web pool game drew "the sound effects are barebones" ([Gamezebo](https://www.gamezebo.com/reviews/8-ball-multiplayer-pool-review/)). Cue Club's audio is its weakest premium cue. The clack is 18 ms of heavily low-passed noise played at constant volume. The pocket is a two-tone sine that reads as a UI jingle. **Cushion events are logged by the solver but never sounded.** The event log stores no impact speed, so nothing can scale with force. The fix is cheap. Add a quantised speed to `CueSim._log`, which appends ints only and changes no physics. Map volume and brightness to speed, and sound the cushions. Swap the synthesis for a small licensed sample set: 4–6 clicks, 3 cushion thuds, 3 pocket drops including a rattle, and a rolling loop, under 300 KB as OGG. Commercial packs of this kind cost $10–20 ([Gravity Sound, itch.io](https://gravity-sound.itch.io/billiard-sound-effects); [Unity Asset Store](https://assetstore.unity.com/packages/audio/sound-fx/foley/billiard-sound-effects-220821)), and their licences go in ASSETS.md.

The second missing beat is the strike. The cue vanishes instead of driving through the ball. Reviewers single out a visible strike with "chalk powder bursting into the air" and slow motion on the winning shot ([Cubed3, 2021](https://www.cubed3.com/review/6380/1/pure-pool-nintendo-switch.html); [MoviesGamesAndTech, 2026](https://moviesgamesandtech.com/2026/02/12/review-pure-pool-pro)). They also complain when replays or slow motion fire on every shot ([App Store, Snooker Stars](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1137235888)). A 60–100 ms forward stroke, scaled by power and synced with the tip sound and haptic on the contact frame, is a low-effort fix. Slow motion should run only on the decisive 8-ball, as a render-side replay of states the deterministic solver already knows, so physics is never scaled by delta. Visuals need less work. A screen-fixed lamp reflection on the balls (matcap-style, still unshaded), a darker contact-shadow core and pocket depth would deliver the "lights reflectively bouncing off the balls" look reviewers notice ([Cubed3](https://www.cubed3.com/review/6380/1/pure-pool-nintendo-switch.html)) at almost no GPU cost. Haptics should follow Android's guidance: 10–20 ms "rich" clicks rather than buzzy one-shots, with "no haptics" preferred over buzzy ones ([Android Developers: haptics principles](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/haptics/haptics-principles)). In practice that means passing Godot's `amplitude` argument ([Godot Input docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/classes/class_input.html)), scaling the strike by power, giving fouls a double "reject" pulse, and keeping ball-ball contacts silent to the hand.

Feature depth is the other visible gap. Practice/free play with no rules, at least two rule sets, and cue and cloth cosmetics appear in almost every well-rated offline app ([Real Pool 3D, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/real-pool-3d/id1156081906); [Pro Pool 2026](https://apps.apple.com/au/app/pro-pool-2024/id556429076)). A free table with cue-ball reposition and "replay last shot" is the cheapest of these here, because the solver re-runs exactly from a saved state. Reviewers name it as missing even from a strong simulation app ([Pro Snooker & Pool listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/pro-snooker-pool-2024/id1586517737)). Next in value are a potted-ball tray with a fly-in animation and an end-of-rack summary card (pot %, longest run, best shot, Rematch). After those come race-to-2/3 frames, a short ladder of named CPU personalities built as parameter presets (original names only), and table themes for the hub shop. A second rule set (9-ball) and pass-and-play are also cheap in a turn-based game, but in a hub of eight games they are retention features, not launch requirements.

## Verdict: hold for one sprint, then closed-test

**Cue Club is not good enough to ship publicly today. It is close, and the blockers are bounded.** It is good enough for internal testers. Four blockers stand between it and a closed-test release. The first is **dead breaks**: casual-power breaks are dry 75–97% of the time, and a full-power break scratches 15% of the time. Every rack opens with that shot, and "breaks are boring" is the most likely first impression. The second is **unmeasured CPU difficulty**. The current σ values came from one session of feel, the analytic formula says Easy is already very weak, and the "too strong" feeling has a better explanation in lucky leaves and straight-shot ball in hand. Shipping without self-play numbers risks the "rigged" reviews that dominate CPU complaints. The third is **the Easy snap trapping small cuts**. Beginners will see straightforward pots miss and blame the game. The fourth is **silent cushions and uniform clicks**. These cost under a day to fix, and sound is the element reviewers praise most. The livelier rails (item 3) ride along with the break fix because the two interact.

The steelman for shipping now is real. Controls are sound, rules are complete and tested, performance has headroom, and the game is offline with no ads, which avoids the top complaint themes in the survey ([Pocket Run Pool, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/ca/app/pocket-run-pool/id949982914); [Real Pool 3D, App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/real-pool-3d/id1156081906)). A hub mini-game is judged on the quality of a three-minute session, not on feature count. That argument justifies leaving out practice mode, 9-ball, cosmetics and the physics refinements. It does not justify leaving out the break or the CPU, because those are the session. Serpent Grid is the first launch title, so Cue Club costs nothing by waiting one sprint and arriving as an update.

## A test protocol with pass targets

Run four layers. Physics tests run headless as gdUnit4 tests that build a `CueSim`, place balls and run to rest. Tests that fail today go in as tracking tests until the retune lands, so the suite stays green per the definition of done. CPU tests run through the `ai-opponent` skill against Greedy-σ reference agents, using **400 paired-seed games per pairing** (SD 2.5%, the convention in [IJCAI 2009](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/09/Papers/231.pdf)). Controls and presentation are checked on the test phone. A 3–5 person playtest anchors the human win rates, because no published casual baseline exists.

## Conclusion

Cue Club's weak spots are mostly in what happens after the shot, not in the controls or the rules. The control decisions that reviews punish elsewhere were settled correctly early, and the solver is accurate enough that its remaining errors (throw curves, speed-invariant rails) matter only to experts. What will decide reviews is three moments: the opening break, the CPU's misses, and the sound of a ball striking a ball. All three are cheap to fix and easy to measure, because the solver is deterministic and every target above can become an exact regression bound. The research also changes how the "CPU too strong" feedback should be read. The fix is to make the CPU's mistakes look human and its leaves look random, not to make it miss more. A CPU that plays position badly but visibly will feel fairer than one that plays no position and gets lucky.

The way to compete while staying original is to use what Cue Club has that pay-to-win online apps do not. Long guidelines cost nothing competitively when there is no online play. Replay and practice are nearly free with a deterministic solver. Daily preset-break puzzles fit the existing seeded daily challenges. A ladder of CPU personalities built from parameter presets gives players a reason to come back without any online play. None of these copies a commercial game's layout or identity, and each builds on the codebase's main strength: exact, replayable physics.
