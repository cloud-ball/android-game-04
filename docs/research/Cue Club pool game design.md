# Analytic physics and Gaussian noise shape Cue Club

Cue Club should run on a closed-form "phase" solver (sliding, rolling, spinning, stationary). Each 1/240 s substep advances every ball with exact piecewise-polynomial equations, then resolves impulses at swept time-of-impact events in a fixed tie order, with all state held in 64-bit `PackedFloat64Array`s. That design makes friction frame-rate independent by construction and makes the solver cheap enough to step live. The real cost problem is the CPU opponent. The plan's "Hard N=128 plus two-ply in ~150 ms" is roughly 30–100× over what interpreted GDScript can do on a mid-range phone. The CPU should instead prune candidates analytically, simulate about 10/40/100 shots (Easy/Medium/Hard) on worker threads, and hide 1–3 s of thinking behind the camera. Difficulty should come mainly from Gaussian aim noise (σ ≈ 0.6°/0.26°/0.12°), not from search depth, because the computational-pool literature shows execution skill outweighs strategy. The rules should be WPA 2025 8-ball with every human option fixed to a default, slop shots allowed, a pocket call only for the 8, and ball in hand anywhere after every foul. Twenty rules cover this, and they are evaluated in a fixed order by a pure function. On screen, the table runs long-axis vertical with no rotation while aiming. Controls are angular drag aim, a fine-aim strip, an eased pull-down power bar and an enlarging spin panel. The whole scene renders in about 12 draw calls with no real lights or shadow maps. Several numbers in `docs/games/pool.md` should change before planning: the rolling-deceleration formula, the CPU noise model and budget, the fine-aim placement, the follow camera, two test tolerances and the scope-cut fallback. A 7-ft playfield should replace the 9-ft one to make balls 28% larger on screen.

## Closed-form ball phases make 1/240 s substeps exact

### Four motion phases have exact equations, so friction never depends on step size

A ball on cloth is always in one of four phases, each with closed-form equations ([Kiefl, pooltool theory](https://ekiefl.github.io/2020/04/24/pooltool-theory/)). **Sliding** applies whenever the contact-point slip velocity u is non-zero. Friction then acts along the initial slip direction û₀, which stays fixed. Acceleration is the constant vector −μs·g·û₀, the path is an exact parabola, and slip decays as u(t) = u₀ − (7/2)μs·g·t. **Rolling** has u = 0, in-plane spin locked to |v|/R, and deceleration μr·g along v. **Spinning** has v = 0 while the vertical-axis spin ω_z decays. **Stationary** is fully at rest. The transition times are simple ratios: sliding→rolling after **Δt = (2/7)·u₀/(μs·g)**, rolling→stop after **Δt = v₀/(μr·g)**, and spinning→stop after Δt = 2R·ω_z/(5·μsp·g) ([Kiefl, pooltool algorithm](https://ekiefl.github.io/2020/12/20/pooltool-alg/)). Sliding→stationary is rare and is treated as two transitions in a row.

The solver should store a phase per ball and, on each substep of remaining time τ, compute the time to that ball's next transition, advance by min(τ, t_transition) with the closed form, and snap state exactly when a transition fires: on slide→roll set u = 0 and ω_xy = (k̂ × v)/R; on roll→stop set v = 0. This is exact for any dt. Step size therefore affects only collision timing, never friction, which is the frame-rate-dependence pitfall `pool.md` names. Two consequences make good unit tests. A stun ball at 2 m/s slides **0.291 s and 0.499 m, then rolls at 5/7·v₀ = 1.43 m/s**. A stopped ball with backspin R·ω rolls back at **(2/7)·R·ω**, which is the "DRAW SHOT" magnitude in the concept clip. Both figures are computed from the cited equations. A cushion hit must put a rolling ball back into sliding: high-speed footage shows it slides and then re-acquires roll ([Mathavan et al. 2009](https://forums.azbilliards.com/attachments/application_of_high-speed_imaging_to_determine_the-pdf.691242/)). With that rule, the bending path after a rail with english falls out of the same sliding equations.

Fully event-based simulation (jump straight to the next event) is the research standard. A typical shot has 10–50 events, against roughly 30,000 fixed steps for 0.3 mm accuracy with naive integration ([Kiefl, pooltool algorithm](https://ekiefl.github.io/2020/12/20/pooltool-alg/); [Leckie & Greenspan 2006](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/an-event-based-pool-physics-simulator/1276086)). Cue Club's hybrid of fixed 1/240 s substeps, analytic motion inside each one and swept events within the substep keeps the repo's fixed-grid rule while getting most of the event method's accuracy. It produces different rounding from a pure event solver, though, so live play and AI search must use the same code path, or the CPU's predictions drift from what the player sees.

### Six cloth and ball constants, and one formula in pool.md is wrong

`pool.md` lists "Rolling deceleration = 5/7 · μr · g". Both Alciatore's convention and pooltool's define μr as deceleration/g, so **rolling deceleration is μr·g with no 5/7 factor** ([Alciatore TP B.2](https://billiards.colostate.edu/technical_proofs/new/TP_B-2.pdf); [Kiefl](https://ekiefl.github.io/2020/04/24/pooltool-theory/)). At μr = 0.012 the correct value is **0.118 m/s²**. Mathavan's high-speed measurement gives 0.124–0.126 m/s² (μr 0.0127–0.0129, constant with speed) ([Mathavan et al. 2009](https://forums.azbilliards.com/attachments/application_of_high-speed_imaging_to_determine_the-pdf.691242/)). The repo formula would give 0.084 m/s², and every rolling ball would travel 40% too far. Fix this in `pool.md` and `.claude/rules/cue-sim.md` before the golden master is frozen. The rest of the parameter set is well supported.

The cue strike can be a single rigid-body impulse with a horizontal cue. A strike at tip offset d gives v = J/m and |R·ω| = (5/2)(d/R)·v. A hit 0.4R above centre therefore produces natural roll immediately, matching Mathavan's observation that a ball "starts to roll immediately only when … hit horizontally at a height of 7R/5" ([Mathavan 2009](https://forums.azbilliards.com/attachments/application_of_high-speed_imaging_to_determine_the-pdf.691242/)). With a horizontal cue the initial slip is collinear with v, so **the cue ball travels straight until it hits something**. That keeps the aim line honest and leaves swerve out of the model at no cost.

Several derived figures should shape test design and pacing. A ball rolling at 1 m/s travels **4.25 m and takes 8.5 s to stop**. Long rolling tails are therefore the norm, so a tap-to-fast-forward control is needed. The stop-shot test in `pool.md` ("cue ball within 1 cm") also needs a speed attached. With e = 0.93 a straight stun leaves the cue ball creeping forward at (1−e)/2·v. It then rolls **0.27 cm after a 1 m/s shot, 0.60 cm at 1.5 m/s and 1.06 cm at 2 m/s** (computed). So the test should run at ≤1.5 m/s or use a tolerance derived from e.

### One impulse rule produces throw, and Han's cushion model produces english

Ball–ball contact should be a two-part impulse. The normal impulse is Jn = (1+e)·(m/2)·v_n along the line of centres. The tangential friction impulse is capped by both Coulomb friction and the point at which slip stops: **Jt = min(μ·Jn, m·|s|/7)**, where s is the contact-point slip velocity including both balls' spin. The 1/7 comes from the effective mass of a contact point on two solid spheres. This generalises Alciatore's throw analysis ([TP A.14](https://billiards.colostate.edu/technical_proofs/new/TP_A-14.pdf)) to two moving, spinning balls, and the slip cap guarantees friction never reverses slip, so energy cannot increase. One rule then produces cut-induced throw, spin-induced throw and zero throw for "gearing" outside english. Alciatore fitted ball–ball friction to Marlow's data as **μ(v) = 0.00995 + 0.108·e^(−1.088·v)** ([TP A.14](https://billiards.colostate.edu/technical_proofs/new/TP_A-14.pdf)). That makes slow shots throw more than fast ones. Computed from that model, a slow 0.447 m/s stun throws about 4.7° at a 30° cut, while a 1.34 m/s rolling hit throws about 1.0°. Ship the constant 0.055 first. Adopt μ(v) only with a deterministic `exp` approximation (see determinism below).

Throw has a UX consequence the plan does not yet address. In section 4's tolerance table, a 0.5 m + 0.5 m half-ball cut allows only about ±0.4° of aim error. A 4.7° throw is therefore ten times the pocket's tolerance. A player who lines up the ghost-ball guideline on a soft cut will miss and blame the game. The fix is to compute the object-ball guideline ray with the solver's own collision function, applied to the cue ball's predicted state at contact. The cue ball's spin at contact is analytic, because it travels straight in a known phase. On Easy and Medium the guideline then shows where the ball will actually go. On Hard, showing only the geometric ghost-ball line turns throw into a learnable skill. If testers still call pots unfair, the notes suggest scaling μ_b by 0.5–1.0.

For cushions, use **Han 2005**. It is a closed-form, instantaneous impulse of about 30 flops. It handles the nose contact angle, spin, and both sliding and sticking, so running english, reverse english and follow/draw all change the rebound angle ([Kiefl, pooltool theory](https://ekiefl.github.io/2020/04/24/pooltool-theory/)). pooltool reports two errors in Han's published paper, and the blog's printed ω equations contain a typo, so **implement from pooltool's corrected source or worksheet, not from the blog text**. Han produces a vertical velocity (the ball hops). Cue Club is 2.5D, so clamp v_z' to 0; this only removes energy. Mathavan's 2010 model is more accurate, but it integrates the impact numerically over up to 1,000 impulse steps ([pooltool mathavan_2010](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ekiefl/pooltool/main/pooltool/physics/resolve/ball_cushion/mathavan_2010/model.py)). Keep it as an offline reference only. If Han slips, a fallback is the same model at θ = 0: v_n' = −e·v_n, plus a tangential impulse min(μ_c(1+e)m|v_n|, (2/7)m|s|). It keeps the sidespin effects on rebound angle and loses only the topspin coupling.

The "0.82 measured vs 0.98 in one model" conflict in `pool.md` mostly dissolves on inspection. The 0.818 is a linear fit to snooker cushions over 0.28–3.5 m/s ([Mathavan 2009](https://forums.azbilliards.com/attachments/application_of_high-speed_imaging_to_determine_the-pdf.691242/)). The 0.98 is a restitution parameter inside Mathavan's two-contact model, which loses additional energy to friction at both the cushion and the cloth ([Mathavan 2010](https://forums.azbilliards.com/attachments/smathavanetalimechemes-pdf.690515/)). The two are not the same quantity, and no pool-specific cushion measurement was found. Keep **e_c = 0.85**. If cushions feel dead on soft shots, add the optional speed-dependent curve e(x) = (−0.0877x² + 1.131x − 0.0953)/x, clamped to [0.5, 0.95]. It gives about 0.77 at 0.28 m/s, 0.95 at 1 m/s and 0.84 at 3 m/s (computed). WPA's requirement that cushions let a ball travel 4–4½ table lengths ([poolroom.com WPA](https://www.poolroom.com/?p=3677)) gives a useful combined calibration target for e_c and μr in a `physics-tune` sweep. The notes do not record the test stroke speed, so treat it as a feel check rather than an exact number.

### What to skip: massé, jump and swerve go; squirt stays a hidden-off tunable

Massé and jump need a cue-elevation input and an airborne 3D phase, so cut both. Swerve without squirt or cue elevation is invisible, as shown above. Squirt is one line of code. Alciatore's formula gives about 3.5° of cue-ball deflection at 0.5R side offset for a high-squirt cue and 1.4° for a low-squirt shaft ([TP A.31](https://billiards.colostate.edu/technical_proofs/new/TP_A-31.pdf)). That is far larger than the pocket tolerances in section 4, so silent squirt would be a hidden aiming penalty. Ship it at 0. Turn it on only in an expert mode where the aim line shows it. Skip ball-ball "cling" as well. The break needs no special physics: sequential pairwise impulses in fixed order give a deterministic spread, and a seeded rack jitter of **0–0.2 mm gaps** from `core/Rng.gd` provides variety. The jitter range is a design choice; no data on rack gaps was found.

### Pockets are cushions plus a capture circle, so rattles come for free

Each pocket should be built from parts the solver already handles. Two jaw-tip arcs (circles of radius about 2.1 cm at corners and 0.8 cm at sides) set the mouth gap. Two straight facings run into the pocket at the WPA cut angles of **142° (corner) and 104° (side)**. Behind the mouth sits a capture circle of radius about 6.2–6.5 cm, at roughly one shelf depth (corner about 4.2 cm, side about 6.9 cm) ([poolroom.com WPA](https://www.poolroom.com/?p=3677); [pooltool table specs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ekiefl/pooltool/main/pooltool/objects/table/specs.py)). A ball is potted on the first substep its centre enters the capture circle. Jaws and facings use the Han cushion code, so rattles and jawed balls emerge without special cases. WPA mouth widths are **11.43–11.75 cm for corners (about two ball diameters) and 12.7–13.0 cm for sides**. pooltool's 7-ft defaults are 11.8 cm and 13.7 cm. Use 11.8 cm and 13.0 cm for casual play. Mouth width is the cleanest difficulty lever, so keep it as per-table data, with a tighter "pro" table for Hard and practice. How WPA's 142°/104° angles are measured needs checking against a diagram before modelling; the notes flag pooltool's 5.3°/7.14° "pocket angle" as a different convention. The vertical back-draft and the drop into the pocket are animation only.

Table size is a design call with a strong readability case for change. `pool.md` specifies a 9-ft playfield (2.54 × 1.27 m). A 7-ft bar-box playfield (1.981 × 0.991 m, pooltool's default) has the same 2:1 aspect, so it fills the portrait screen identically. Balls on it render **20.6 dp instead of 16.1 dp** across (computed: 53 vs 41 canvas px), which is 28% larger, and typical shots are shorter, so pot rates rise for casual players. Every physical constant above is size-independent. Recommend 7-ft unless the keyframes' proportions are a hard requirement.

### Swept tests, a fixed tie order and 64-bit scalars keep it deterministic

Swept tests are mandatory. At a 12 m/s break a ball moves **5 cm per 1/240 s substep**, more than R (2.86 cm), so overlap tests would tunnel. Inside a substep, the friction curvature of the path is at most ½·μs·g·dt² ≈ 0.017 mm (computed), so a linear swept-circle quadratic is accurate enough. For balls A and B with d = p_B − p_A and w = v_B − v_A, solve a·t² + 2b·t + c = 0 with a = w·w, b = d·w and c = d·d − 4R². A hit requires b < 0 and b² − a·c ≥ 0. Take **t = c / (−b + √(b² − a·c))**, the cancellation-free form. If c < 0 already (overlap from round-off) and b < 0, resolve at t = 0 rather than skipping. Cushion facings use distance-to-line minus R with an on-segment check. Jaw arcs are circles of radius R + r_jaw, solved with the same quadratic. Godot vectors cannot hold this state, because "float is 64-bit in GDScript, but Vector2, Vector3 and Vector4 are 32-bit" ([Godot docs, large world coordinates](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/physics/large_world_coordinates.html)). Store px, py, vx, vy, ωx, ωy, ωz and phase in preallocated `PackedFloat64Array`s. That also satisfies the repo rule against per-frame allocations.

The per-substep loop is short:

```
τ = 1/240
loop (max 64 events, then positional separation fallback):
    t* = earliest of {ball–ball TOI, ball–cushion/jaw TOI, capture-circle entry, phase transition}
    if t* > τ: advance all moving balls by τ analytically; break
    advance all moving balls by t*; resolve the event(s) at t*; τ -= t*
```

Treat events within 1e-12 s of each other as simultaneous. Resolve them sequentially by lowest ball index, then the second index, and re-check approach (v_n > 1e-9 m/s) before each one. This Gauss–Seidel ordering is deterministic and handles frozen clusters and the rack. pooltool itself models only pairwise collisions ([Kiefl](https://ekiefl.github.io/2020/04/24/pooltool-theory/)). After resolving, separate any pair within 1e-9 m of overlap symmetrically along the normal. Iterate a list of moving balls rather than all pairs: in a local benchmark run for these notes, an all-pairs loop that skipped only static–static pairs barely beat full cost.

Cross-platform determinism has one real hazard: transcendental functions. IEEE-754 +, −, ×, ÷ and √ are correctly rounded and identical on x86 and ARM when evaluation order is fixed. `sin`, `cos`, `exp` and `atan2` come from the platform libm and may differ in the last bit between desktop and Android. The notes flag this as general numerical knowledge, not verified for Godot 4.7. Keep transcendentals out of the hot path. Represent the shot direction as a unit vector computed once at the input boundary, not as an angle the sim converts with `cos`/`sin`. Store Han's sinθ/cosθ as literal constants. Use either the constant μ_b or a rational approximation of exp built from basic operations. Otherwise accept per-platform golden files; generating one on desktop and one on the test phone resolves the open question empirically. On one device, AI search and live play are bit-identical either way.

The test list in `pool.md` should grow from four to nine, all pure-logic GdUnit4 tests. Energy never increases: E_after ≤ E_before·(1 + 1e-12) after every event. Phase timing: a stun at v₀ rolls at exactly 2v₀/(7μs·g) with speed 5v₀/7. Draw-back speed is (2/7)·R·ω. The 90° rule: with e = 1 and μ = 0, the post-collision velocities of a stun cut are perpendicular. The 30° rule: a natural-roll half-ball hit deflects the cue ball about 27–34°, a wide-tolerance inferred check. Outside gearing english gives about zero throw. No overlaps over 10,000 random shots: minimum centre distance ≥ 2R − 1e-9. The golden-master break matches to 1e-6, with per-platform goldens if needed. Frame-rate independence: the same shot rendered at 30, 60 and 120 fps gives a bitwise-identical end state. Running cue_sim inside `_physics_process` at 60 ticks with exactly four substeps per tick makes the last test trivially true.

## Portrait touch controls keep the table fixed and the thumbs off the balls

### A vertical, fixed table is the only layout that keeps 16 balls readable

On the 420 dp test phone, a vertical long axis gives a playfield short side of about 357 dp (85% of width) and a 9-ft ball of about 16 dp (2.5 mm). Horizontal would halve that to 8 dp, too small for numbers or stripes (derived from the device geometry). Target the playfield at **85–90% of width and about 75% of height**. Reserve about **90–110 dp at the top for the HUD** (turn and groups) and **60–80 dp at the bottom** for hints, and overlay side controls on the rails rather than shrinking the table. The local keyframe already shows this layout. Top-down is the genre default on mobile ([Pocket Gamer review](https://www.pocketgamer.com/pool/review/)). Do **not rotate the table to follow the aim**. Accessibility guidance says to "avoid … any difference between controller movement and camera movement" and "any sudden unexpected movement" ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)), and a fixed table keeps drag directions stable.

### Angular drag for coarse aim, a separate strip for fine aim, and a lift-off filter

Two aiming conventions dominate the genre. Coarse aim is a drag on the table that rotates the cue around the cue ball. Fine aim uses a separate high-resolution control or a hold-to-enter fine mode ([FlyOrDie 3D Billiards help](https://www.flyordie.com/games/help/billiard/en/contents.html)). The best-documented failure is the aim being nudged as the finger lifts: a reviewer reports "you sometimes nudge the aiming line" on release, and small-step buttons fix it ([Pocket Gamer review](https://www.pocketgamer.com/pool/review/)). Touch research explains why. Fingers occlude their targets ([Vogel & Baudisch, CHI 2007](https://projects.csail.mit.edu/hci-reading/papers/chi2007-vogel.pdf)), and the sensed contact centre sits about 4 mm from where users think they touched ([Holz & Baudisch, Understanding Touch](https://siplab.org/projects/Understanding_Touch)).

Cue Club should map coarse drag to **the angle the finger sweeps around the cue ball's screen position**, not to raw horizontal delta. A finger far from the cue ball then acts as a longer lever and gives finer control for free. On release, discard the last **50–80 ms** of motion, or freeze aim once finger speed has stayed below a threshold for about 100 ms. The fine-aim strip should give about **0.03° of cue rotation per dp** (range 0.02–0.05°), so a 300 dp stroke covers about 9°. Touch accuracy at screen edges is about **11–12 mm**, against about 7 mm at the centre ([Smashing Magazine target-size cheatsheet](https://smashingmagazine.com/2023/04/accessible-tap-target-sizes-rage-taps-clicks/)), so the strip's hit area should be **at least 72 dp wide** even if it is drawn thinner. Expose a sensitivity setting, as accessibility guidance asks ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)), and a left-handed mirror. Placement conflicts with the plan. `pool.md` puts fine aim on the right edge, but the keyframe puts the power bar there and the spin inset on the left. Put fine aim as a vertical strip on the left edge below the spin inset, or give the two right-edge controls a clear gap of at least 8 dp. Tap-to-aim on a ball is a useful accelerator, but at 10–18 dp of touch error it should mean "aim near here", not set the final aim. An optional magnetic snap of about 0.5° to the nearest legal ball's centre-pocket line can serve as an Easy-only assist. All of these values are inferences; no commercial sensitivity figures are published.

### Guidelines should show physics truth, and their length is the assist dial

The ghost ball, "the imaginary place the CB needs to be at OB contact", is the standard aiming concept and the core on-screen guide ([Dr. Dave aiming terminology](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/?p=537)). Render four elements. A dotted cue-ball path runs from the cue ball to first contact. A ghost-ball outline at least one ball diameter across, with a 1.5–2 dp outline, marks the contact. An object-ball direction ray comes off the contact. A cue-ball deflection ray shows the follow-on path: roughly 90° to the object ball for a stun, bending toward about 30° for a rolling ball. Distinguish the lines by dash pattern and width, not colour alone. As argued above, compute the object-ball and deflection rays from the solver's own collision function, so they include throw and spin. Guideline length is the natural assist dial. Easy shows the full object-ball ray to the pocket or cushion plus the spin-bent deflection. Medium shows about a quarter table of object-ball ray and a short deflection ray. Hard shows the cue path and ghost ball with a two-diameter object ray. No source documents commercial lengths, so these are starting values. Keep "long guidelines" as an accessibility toggle separate from CPU difficulty.

### An eased pull-down power bar spans 0.5 to 8 m/s, with 12 m/s reserved for the break

Real cue-ball speeds run from slow (0.45–0.89 m/s) through medium (0.89–1.9 m/s) and fast (1.9–3.2 m/s) to a powerful break at 11–13 m/s ([Dr. Dave FAQ: typical speeds](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/faq/speed/typical/)). A linear bar to break speed would squeeze nearly every useful shot into its bottom fifth. Map **v = v_max·p^γ with γ = 2, v_max = 8 m/s for normal shots and 12 m/s on the break**. A medium 1.5 m/s shot then sits at about 43% of the bar instead of 19% (computed), and showing a percentage or speed tick next to the bar makes the curve learnable. The power bar is a dedicated vertical strip at the screen edge, an established convention ([Pocket Gamer review](https://www.pocketgamer.com/pool/review/)). Make it **280–360 dp long** with a 48 dp touch strip (72 dp at the edge), per Android's 48 dp minimum ([Google Accessibility Help](https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/7101858?hl=en)). Pull down (away from the shot) to load power and release to fire. Dragging back under about 2% cancels with a visible "×". Lock aim during the power drag, and fire with the value sampled about 50 ms before release. Avoid an oscillating timing meter; "do not make precise timing essential" is an explicit guideline ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)).

### Spin needs an enlarging panel, and vertical spin is the part that cannot be cut

A reviewer called the lack of spin "far more disappointing" than other flaws, something "you'd think would be essential" ([Pocket Gamer review](https://www.pocketgamer.com/pool/review/)). Spin also carries the concept clip's hero moment, a draw shot. A 96–120 dp cue-ball inset gives only a 48–60 dp usable dot area within 0.5R. That is about 3×3 distinguishable positions under 7 mm finger error. Tapping the inset should therefore open a **200–240 dp spin panel**, where the dot moves by relative drag at **0.5 gain** inside a faint 0.5R limit ring. Add one-tap preset chips (Centre, Follow, Draw, Stun, Left, Right), each at least 48 dp with text labels, and reset to centre after each shot. This changes the scope-cut fallback in `pool.md`. Ship **vertical spin only (Follow, Stun, Draw)** if behind, not centre-ball only. Side english touches only throw and the cushion model, while vertical spin is the draw-back demo.

### Ball in hand: indirect drag, clamping to legal space, and commit on aim

No public UX writeups exist for ball-in-hand placement, so these are inferences from touch research. Grab within about 48 dp of the cue ball. Move it by **relative drag**, or render it 40–60 dp above the finger (a Shift-style offset), so the thumb never hides it ([Vogel & Baudisch](https://projects.csail.mit.edu/hci-reading/papers/chi2007-vogel.pdf)). Tint the legal region, which is the whole bed under the casual rules below, and slide the ghost cue ball along obstacles at 2R + ε rather than rejecting a drop. Commit the placement only when the player starts aiming, and show an "aim from here" preview while placing.

### Camera moves happen only after release, and never follow the fastest ball

Aiming and power always happen in the fixed overview, so the touch-to-world mapping stays linear. The low stroke camera should transition in **0.4–0.6 s with ease-in-out, only after release**, and return within about 0.5 s of the last ball slowing. The plan's "follow cam on the fastest ball" will lurch on breaks, because the fastest ball changes identity constantly. Instead follow a **damped centroid of moving balls** (time constant 0.25–0.4 s) or stay top-down on breaks. Clamp camera angular velocity to 90–120°/s, never roll, and provide a "Reduce camera motion" toggle that keeps the overview throughout, as the guidelines require ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)). Pinch zoom conflicts with drag-anywhere aim, so use a zoom button (1–2×) instead. No data on motion sickness in mobile sports cameras was found, so these thresholds come from general accessibility guidance.

### Readability needs numbers, patterns and outlines, never colour alone

At 16–21 dp per ball, a real number disc (about a third of the diameter) would be under 1 mm and illegible. Render numbers as an upright, camera-facing label at **55–60% of ball diameter** with a "Show numbers" toggle on by default, and verify on the device. Keep stripe bands at about 50% of diameter so the white caps read from above. Give the cue ball a subtle marker so it is distinguishable from stripe caps. Colour-blind players already ask pool games for "numbers to always be on top" or distinct patterns ([Combo Pool itch.io comments](https://nusan.itch.io/combo-pool/comments?after=59)), and guidelines forbid conveying essential information "by a fixed colour alone" ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)). The keyframe's HUD shows groups as coloured dots only, which needs a "Stripes 9–15" label or number chips. Give every ball and guide line **at least 3:1 contrast against the cloth**, the WCAG non-text threshold ([W3C SC 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). Add a 1–1.5 dp rim where dark balls (black, maroon, dark green, purple) fall short on teal cloth. Draw guides 1.5–2 dp thick in near-white.

### Feedback: crisp haptics on discrete events, rate-limited clicks, explained fouls

Android's haptics guidance favours "rich and clear haptics over buzzy" ones, says keyclicks should last **10–20 ms**, and says "given the choice of buzzy haptics or no haptics … choose no haptics" ([Android Developers: haptics principles](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/haptics/haptics-principles)). Godot's `Input.vibrate_handheld` is a one-shot vibration of the kind that guidance discourages, so keep pulses at 10–20 ms and low amplitude, or wrap the platform constants in a small plugin. Map events this way: light ticks every 10% of power, rising toward 100%; one crisp strike pulse scaled by power; one firm pulse per pot; a double "reject" tick on a foul; a short pattern on a win; and nothing on ball–ball contacts, which are far too frequent on breaks. Provide a toggle plus an intensity slider. For sound, scale clicks by relative speed, cap at about 8 simultaneous voices and ignore contacts under about 0.05 m/s. Shot-result banners should last 1.0–1.5 s, not block play, and explain fouls in words ("Foul: no ball hit a cushion"), at 16–18 sp and 4.5:1 contrast. Leave the shot clock **off** against the CPU. After release, lock out table touches for 0.3–0.5 s and while balls move, apart from tap-to-fast-forward.

## Twenty fixed rules turn WPA 8-ball into a prompt-free state machine

### Official rules already contain most "mobile simplifications"

The current WPA text (effective 2025-09-15) is closer to casual play than its reputation suggests. Every standard foul gives **ball in hand anywhere**. The three-foul rule explicitly does not apply ("Some games such as 8-Ball do not include this rule"). An 8 potted on a legal break is neither a win nor a loss. The four ways to lose are potting the 8 on a foul, before your group is cleared, in an uncalled pocket, or driving it off the table ([WPA Rules of Play 2025](https://www.hkbilliardsports.org.hk/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/2025.09.15-WPA-Rules-WPA-Regs-NP.pdf)). UK blackball's governing bodies moved from the two-visit penalty to **one visit with ball in hand anywhere** in 2022 ([Wikipedia: Blackball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackball_(pool))). Mainstream rules on both sides of the Atlantic now agree on the simplest penalty. The real departures a casual vs-CPU game needs are four. First, allow slop: only the 8 needs a called pocket, an established league convention ([Dr. Dave: league rule differences](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/faq/rules/league-rule-differences/)). Second, fix defaults for WPA §4.3's five break-option prompts. Third, give ball in hand anywhere even after break fouls, as CSI/BCAPL do. Fourth, drop enforcement of the four-balls-to-rail legal break. Physical fouls (foot on floor, double hit, push shot, touched ball) cannot happen in a simulated stroke. Neither can off-table balls in a 2.5D solver that clamps v_z, so those rules stay in the engine for completeness but are unreachable in v1.

The two most consequential choices are R4 and R6. Bar rules often make an 8 on the break an instant win. Against a CPU, that lets a random break decide the whole session, so always re-spot. The 8 call costs one tap per game but prevents the most frustrating casual outcome: winning or losing on an accidental 8 drop. The rail rule (R10) is the least visible rule to casual players. It stays mainly so the CPU cannot exploit soft-tap safeties, and the foul banner must say why it fired.

### The engine is one pure function with a fixed evaluation order

Most edge cases come from combining one shot's events (first contact, rails after contact, potted set, cue fate) with three pieces of state: open or assigned table, whether the shooter's group was cleared *at shot start*, and whether this is the break. "Cleared" must be **snapshotted before the shot**. Checked afterwards, last-ball-plus-8 on the same stroke wrongly becomes a win. The open-table "empty group" exception of WPA §4.4 is reachable in practice, for example when all solids drop across the break and open-table shots. Without it the engine deadlocks or wrongly fouls a shooter who hits the 8.

```
RulesState  {phase: BREAK|PLAY|GAME_OVER, shooter, breaker, group[2]: NONE|SOLIDS|STRIPES,
             on_table: PackedByteArray[16], ball_in_hand: NONE|KITCHEN|ANYWHERE,
             called_pocket: -1..5, shots_without_pot, winner, end_reason}
ShotContext (snapshot at shot start):
  cleared_at_start = open ? (no solids left or no stripes left) : no balls of group[shooter] left
  on_eight = cleared_at_start ; requires_call = on_eight and phase == PLAY   # no call -> reject shot, no foul
ShotEvents (from cue_sim): first_contact (-1 none; legal ball wins a same-tick tie),
  rail_after_contact (new contact-begin at/after first-contact tick), pocketed[{ball,pocket,tick}],
  off_table[], break_rail_balls

apply_shot(state, ctx, events) -> ShotResult        # pure: never mutates state
 1. pots = pocketed objects except 8; eight_down; eight_off; cue_lost
 2. collect fouls: SCRATCH/CUE_OFF | NO_CONTACT | WRONG_BALL (open: hit 8 unless on_eight;
    assigned: not own group; on_eight: not 8) | NO_RAIL (not break, no pots, no 8, contact, no rail)
    | OBJECT_OFF_TABLE
 3. BREAK: respot 8 if down/off; respot off-table balls; foul -> switch, BIH ANYWHERE;
    pots or 8 down -> continue; else switch. Table stays open. phase = PLAY. return
 4. 8 down or off (PLAY) -> GAME_OVER, first match wins:
    EIGHT_OFF_TABLE > EARLY_EIGHT (not cleared_at_start) > EIGHT_WITH_FOUL > WRONG_POCKET > else WIN
 5. respot off-table objects; remove pocketed from on_table
 6. foul -> switch, BIH ANYWHERE, groups unchanged. return
 7. open table and pots -> assign per R8, continue
 8. assigned -> continue if any pot in own group else switch (cue ball where it lies)
 9. shots_without_pot: reset on any pot else +1; at 24 -> RERACK (same breaker)
10. clear called_pocket
```

Invariants for property tests are: exactly one of continue, switch, game-over or re-rack per shot; `group[0] != group[1]` once assigned; balls on table plus balls potted always equals 15; no assignment on a foul or the break; and `apply_shot` is pure. The notes include 40 fixture tests. Ten cover the break, including 8-on-break with and without a scratch and a blocked foot spot during re-spot. Nine cover the open table, including mixed pots, hitting the 8 first, a frozen-rail "no rail" and the empty-group claim. Six cover the assigned table. Ten cover the 8-ball endings, including last-ball-plus-8, a wrong-ball-first 8 pot and a missing call. The last five are meta tests: stalemate, seeded rack determinism and ball conservation. The `rail_after_contact` flag must come from contact-*begin* events. A continuously touching frozen ball must not satisfy it ([WPA §2.7](https://www.hkbilliardsports.org.hk/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/2025.09.15-WPA-Rules-WPA-Regs-NP.pdf)). A shot ends when every ball's translational velocity is zero. A ball still spinning in place cannot change the outcome, so there is no WPA-style settling window. One open item: wpapool.com links a newer Rules of Play PDF dated 2026-10-21 that the research could not read ([wpapool.com](https://wpapool.com/rules-of-play/)). Re-check §4 before shipping.

## Execution noise, not search depth, sets CPU difficulty

### The proven architecture is enumerate, prune, sample, score

The plan's pipeline matches the two computational 8-ball tournament winners. PickPocket generates straight-in shots for every (legal ball, pocket) pair. It varies speed in 1 m/s steps up to 4.5 m/s, plus tip offsets, to create position variants, and falls back to banks, kicks and combinations only when no straight-in exists, which was about 10% of positions ([Smith, AAAI 2006](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2006/AAAI06-156.pdf)). CueCard extended this with 25–100 noisy samples per variant and won 64 of 78 tournament games ([Archibald et al., IJCAI 2009](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/09/Papers/231.pdf)). Two findings reshape the plan. First, **deeper search barely mattered**. CueCard's second search level changed the first-level decision only 18% of the time, and spending its time on more first-level shots was statistically indistinguishable over 1,256 games (51.6% ± 1.4%). Second, **the break was worth about 10% of all wins**, as much as the extra sampling ([IJCAI 2009](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/09/Papers/231.pdf)). The "two-ply lookahead" for Hard should therefore be a heuristic evaluation of the resulting table that costs no simulations. The break should be precomputed offline, not searched at runtime.

Pruning before simulating is what makes mobile feasible. A straight-in shot's difficulty depends on the cut angle α, the object-ball-to-pocket distance d1 and the cue-to-ghost-ball distance d2 ([Smith 2006](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2006/AAAI06-156.pdf)). A small-angle derivation from ghost-ball geometry gives the aim tolerance as **tol ≈ atan(w_eff/2 / d1) / (1 + d2/(2R·cos α))** and the pot probability as **P = erf(tol / (σ_φ·√2))**. Here w_eff is the effective pocket width, about 7.75 cm (3.052 in) for a corner, following Alciatore's rule of subtracting about a ball diameter from the mouth ([Alciatore TP A.2](https://billiards.colostate.edu/technical_proofs/new/TP_A-2.pdf)). The formula is the researcher's own and should be validated against solver pot rates. The CPU should reject candidates with a cut over about 80° or a blocked path (a 2R-wide capsule test on both legs). It then scores the rest by P and simulates only the top **K = 6–10**, at about 0.05 ms per candidate in GDScript. Because ghost-ball aim ignores throw, the CPU should refine its aim with one noiseless 1/240 s simulation of the chosen shot before applying noise.

| Shot (d2, d1, cut) | Aim tolerance | P(pot) at σ 0.12° | 0.26° | 0.6° | 2.5° (≈ plan's Easy) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.3 m, 0.3 m, 0° | ±1.18° | 100% | 100% | 95% | 36% |
| 0.5 m, 0.5 m, 30° | ±0.40° | ~100% | ~88% | 49% | 13% |
| 1.0 m, 1.0 m, 0° | ±0.12° | ~68% | ~36% | 16% | 4% |
| 1.0 m, 1.0 m, 60° | ±0.06° | ~38% | ~18% | 8% | 2% |

The 0.6° and 2.5° columns are the notes' worked values from the formula above; the 0.12° and 0.26° columns were computed here with the same formula. It shows the plan's noise is miscalibrated at both ends. **Easy at ±2.5° pots only about a third of 30 cm straight-ins**, which reads as broken, not easy. **Hard at ±0.3°, if read as σ, is weaker than PickPocket's "strong amateur"** model (σ_φ = 0.185°, which "often misses longer shots") ([Smith 2006](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2006/AAAI06-156.pdf)). The tournament-standard noise was σ_φ = 0.125° and σ_V = 0.075 m/s ([Archibald et al., AAMAS 2010](https://ifaamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2010/pdf/01%20Full%20Papers/22_05_FP_0677.pdf)).

### Noise is the dial, and the CPU must plan with its own noise

Archibald et al. ran about 20,000 games per agent and found that a top planner with noise above 2.5× tournament level loses to a naive shot-finder with perfect execution. Raw execution skill dominates strategy ([AAMAS 2010](https://ifaamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas2010/pdf/01%20Full%20Papers/22_05_FP_0677.pdf)). Use Gaussian noise drawn from `core/Rng.gd`, clamped at 3σ, not a uniform ± bound. Score each candidate with P(pot) at **that level's own σ**, so the CPU picks shots it can actually make. Position play uses PickPocket's evaluation, which CueCard reused unchanged: take the top three distinct next-shot probabilities and combine them as **1.0·p1 + 0.33·p2 + 0.15·p3** (range 0–1.48). The discounts make 90/10/10 roughly equal to 70/70/70 ([Smith 2006](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2006/AAAI06-156.pdf)). For 8-ball, count only the CPU's own group (the 8 once cleared), subtract 1.0 for a scratch, and subtract 0.1 per own ball with no makeable pocket.

| Level | σ aim | σ speed | σ spin | Shot pool | Position play | Safety when best P < | Simulations / turn | Visible think |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | **0.6°** (0.5–0.7) | 8% of V | 0.25R | Straight-ins, weighted-random among top 3 | None (centre ball, 1.3·v_min) | never | ~10 | 1.5–3 s |
| Medium | **0.26°** (0.22–0.30) | 5% | 0.15R | + banks if nothing else | 3 speeds × 3 tips, noiseless | 30% | ~40 | 1.5–3 s |
| Hard | **0.12°** (0.10–0.14) | 3% | 0.08R | + kicks/combos as fallback | Full grid + 16 noisy samples on top 2–3 | 55% | ~100 | 1–2.5 s |
| Expert (unlock) | 0.06° | 2% | 0.05R | as Hard | + heuristic 2-ply | 55% | ~100 | 1–2 s |

Weakening the CPU by noise alone has a known failure. A snooker AI made easier with "random angular deviation" still felt "too good", because players read its random lucky leaves as intent ([West, Game Developer 2009](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/intelligent-mistakes-how-to-incorporate-stupidity-into-your-ai-code)). Add "intelligent mistakes": pick the second- or third-best candidate on about 20% of Easy shots and 8% of Medium shots. Under-hit for position more often than mis-aiming. Never deliberately scratch on the 8. Safety search follows PickPocket. It runs only at the root, when the best offensive P falls below threshold (PickPocket used 65%), and it pays off most against inaccurate opponents ([Smith 2006](https://cdn.aaai.org/AAAI/2006/AAAI06-156.pdf)). Casual humans are such opponents. On mobile, cap it at about 24 (direction, speed) pairs: 12 edge directions × 2 soft speeds that still reach a rail. Score each as −(opponent evaluation) − P(foul)·(ball-in-hand value). For ball in hand, sample placements 0.2–0.5 m behind the ghost ball of low-cut candidates. For the break, sweep cue placement × speed × spin offline with the `physics-tune` skill. Store 2–3 breaks weighted toward **keeping the turn**: CueCard chose a 92% keep-turn break over spread-maximising breaks that succeeded at best about 60% ([IJCAI 2009](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/09/Papers/231.pdf)). Then apply each level's noise. Rubber-banding, if used at all, should be hidden, limited to ±15% on σ_φ from the ball-count gap, and disabled on Hard. Players choosing their own difficulty is the more honest lever.

### 150 ms buys one to four simulations; plan for 1–3 s on worker threads

No published GDScript solver timing exists, so the estimate rests on a local microbenchmark run for these notes (Godot 4.7.2 headless, Apple M1 Pro, toy solver without spin, cushions or pockets). It measured 25 µs per 240 Hz substep with all 16 balls moving and 6.1 µs with 3 moving, about 15 ms per 10 s of simulated time. The notes assume the real solver costs 3–5× the toy and the phone runs 2–3× slower than an M1 Pro. On that basis, one full AI rollout of a typical 4–8 s shot costs **roughly 35–130 ms on the test phone**. The plan's 150 ms budget therefore buys 1–4 rollouts, and N = 128 would take 5–15 s on one core. The same multipliers say the live solver costs only about 0.15–0.4 ms per substep on the phone even on a full break, or 0.6–1.5 ms per 60 Hz tick. Live play is not the bottleneck.

The levers, in order of payoff, are as follows. Analytic pruning turns about 42 (ball, pocket) pairs into 6–10 candidates. Early termination stops a rollout once the target is potted or clearly missed and only the cue ball still rolls, then extrapolates its rest point as v²/(2·μr·g); the notes estimate this saves 50–80% of substeps. A **1/120 s planning step** halves cost. Because motion inside a substep is analytic, the only extra error is the linear-TOI approximation, about 0.07 mm at 1/120 s (computed). That is well below the effect of execution noise, and the chosen shot always executes in the real 1/240 s solver. Coarse-to-fine sampling runs one noiseless rollout per variant, then 6–16 noisy samples only for the top 2–3, using the analytic P(pot) in place of noisy pot sampling. `WorkerThreadPool.add_group_task` spreads the work across about 3 big cores, with per-task preallocated solver buffers and results reduced in candidate-index order so scheduling cannot change the choice. The repo already runs the Stack Drop CPU this way (`games/blocks/scenes/blocks_game.gd`, around line 278). A C++ GDExtension core would be 20–50× faster, but it breaks the pure-GDScript logic rule; keep it as a last resort. **Benchmark µs per substep on the device the day `shared/cue_sim` exists.** Every budget above is an estimate until then.

### Self-play measures difficulty, with targets set in advance

Measure each level headlessly against "Greedy-σ" reference agents (highest-P shot, no position play) at σ_φ = 0.15°, 0.3° and 0.6°, standing in for strong, average and casual humans. Run **400 games per pairing** with alternating breaks, which gives SD = 1/(2√N) = 2.5%, the convention CueCard's analysis used ([IJCAI 2009](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/09/Papers/231.pdf)). Use paired seeds (identical racks for both agents) to cut variance. Proposed targets: Easy beats casual about 50% and average about 30%; Medium beats average about 55%; Hard beats average about 80% and strong about 55%; each level beats the one below 65–75% head-to-head. Also log pot %, balls per visit, scratch %, safeties per game, mean think time, the 95th-percentile decision time on the device, and **shots per rack × mean shot time**, which is the only way to check the 2–5 minute session target. No source measures it. This maps directly onto the repo's `ai-opponent` skill. Later, the method of Archibald & Nieves-Rivera for estimating a player's execution noise from observed misses ([JAIR 2024](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/15326/27033/39091)) could auto-suggest a difficulty level.

## About twelve draw calls render the whole table

### One MultiMesh and one object-space shader draw all sixteen balls

Godot's Mobile renderer does not auto-instance identical meshes ("only implemented in the Forward+ renderer"), so the balls must be a **MultiMesh** ([Godot optimizing 3D](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/optimizing_3d_performance.html)). Choose each ball's pattern from its normalised object-space position rather than UVs. That avoids pole and seam distortion and needs no per-ball textures. A known billiard-ball fragment shader stripes balls where |p.y| > 0.55. It draws a white number disc where length(p.xy) < 0.4 on the ±z hemispheres, flipping x on the back so digits are not mirrored, and reads digits from a **4×4 atlas** ([getlazarus ball shader](https://www.getlazarus.org/pool/balls/)). Cue Club can use the same technique with its own font and colours. Use `fwidth`-based smoothstep edges, store the ball id in `INSTANCE_CUSTOM`, and convert colours with `srgb_to_linear()` as `blocks_view.gd` already does. A 512² atlas of 128 px cells, VRAM-compressed with mipmaps, is ample. Use a **SphereMesh of 32 radial segments × 16 rings**, about 560 vertices per ball and 9k for all 16, instead of the 64×32 default. Silhouette error is under 0.1 px in the overview, and balls stay round in the stroke camera.

Shade in the shader, following the house style (`render_mode unshaded`, linear tonemap). Use Lambert-wrap diffuse from one fixed lamp direction with about 0.25 ambient, and a tight Blinn highlight (exponent 60–120) that lands in the same screen-relative spot on every ball; that consistency is the strongest "pool ball" cue. Add a fake reflection by sampling a hand-painted **128×64 environment strip** (dark room, one rectangular lamp) with the view reflection vector, weighted by Schlick Fresnel 0.04 + 0.96·(1−N·V)⁵, and slightly darken the lower hemisphere in place of SSAO. SSAO, SSR and SSIL are unavailable on Mobile ([Godot renderers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)), and a ReflectionProbe would only help a lit StandardMaterial3D, so skip it. For anti-aliasing, Godot says "FXAA is usually the only viable option" on mobile, while 2× MSAA "may be usable in some circumstances" ([Godot 3D antialiasing](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/3d_antialiasing.html)). Ball silhouettes against flat cloth are the main aliasing source, and FXAA would soften the digits. So default to **MSAA 2×** with an FXAA fallback toggle, measure both on the device, and keep the screen-space roughness limiter on.

### Orientation is visual only, integrated per tick with interpolation off at the root

Keep a per-ball quaternion owned by the renderer and never fed back to the solver. Update it each fixed step as `q = (Quaternion(ω̂, |ω|·dt) * q).normalized()`, with world-frame ω pre-multiplied. For pure rolling on an XZ table with Y up, ω = up × v / R. Unit-test the sign, because a ball rolling backwards is the classic bug. Follow the repo rule. Set `physics_interpolation_mode = OFF` on the game root, keep previous and current position and quaternion per ball, and in `_process` lerp and slerp by `Engine.get_physics_interpolation_fraction()`. Snap pocketed or re-spotted balls with no interpolation ([Godot physics interpolation](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/physics/interpolation/using_physics_interpolation.html)). Fast balls will strobe. At 4 m/s a ball turns **140 rad/s, 2.3 rad per frame at 60 fps and 4.7 rad (more than π) at the 30 fps floor**, so stripes and numbers can appear to spin backwards (computed). Clamp the *visual* rotation to about 1.2 rad per rendered frame, and fade pattern contrast toward the average colour above about 1.5 rad per frame. The 1.2 rad figure is a heuristic to tune on the device.

### The table is painted light: no lights, no shadow maps, no decals

Build the table from 3–5 draws. Each is an unshaded shader with the lamp painted in. The cloth plane gets a radial "pool of light" brightness from 0.78 to 1.10, a 10–15% vignette within 4–6 cm of the cushions, a baked 256² fibre-noise PNG at ±3–4%, and a ±2–3% nap term that shifts tone as the camera moves. Rails, cushions and wood share one mesh with region IDs, procedural grain and a varnish highlight. Pockets are soft black discs plus a rail liner. A pocketed ball drops below the cloth and is clipped by `discard` in the ball shader inside the pocket radius; this is the only discard in the scene, so early-Z is not lost elsewhere. The 18 sights are an SDF in the rail shader at no extra draw cost, as small original mother-of-pearl dots. Shadows are a **MultiMesh of 16 soft quads** offset about 0.15R from the lamp, with a darker contact core. **Do not use Decals for ball shadows**: Mobile allows "only 8 decals" per mesh, and decal cost scales with screen coverage ([Godot using decals](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/using_decals.html)). Skip LightmapGI and real lights. A directional shadow with PSSM draws objects up to five times ([Godot lights and shadows](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/lights_and_shadows.html)), and the painted look is fully art-directable in about 20 lines of shader. Keep glow gentle (house `glow_hdr_threshold` ≈ 1.15, highlight peaks 1.3–1.6), because Mobile's RGB10A2 buffer has little HDR headroom ([Godot renderers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)).

### A long lens at KEEP_WIDTH, pitched 10–15°, frames the table without egg-shaped balls

Godot's docs name `KEEP_WIDTH` as "usually the best option for projects running in portrait mode" ([Godot Camera3D](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_camera3d.html)). Use a **horizontal FOV of 18–26° (start at 22°)**. At 22° the corner balls stretch about 6% (1/cos 19°), while 35° would egg them out by 12–14% (computed). With the outer table filling 92% of width, the camera sits about 4.2 m away for the 9-ft table or **3.4 m for the 7-ft**. Pitch it 10–15° from vertical toward the player, so the far rail narrows slightly as in the keyframes, and centre the projection on the layout's table rect, as `BlocksView.camera_transform` does. The doc's wording about which axis `fov` controls under KEEP_WIDTH is ambiguous, so verify it in the editor first. The stroke camera puts the eye 0.25–0.35 m above the cloth and 0.6–0.9 m behind the cue ball, pitched 12–20° down with a 40–50° horizontal FOV. It looks at a point 0.4–0.6 m past the cue ball and never rolls. Blend eye, look target and FOV separately with critically damped exponential smoothing (k ≈ 6–10 s⁻¹, settling in 0.4–0.6 s) and rebuild the transform with `looking_at`; never lerp a Transform3D directly. Orthographic, as Stack Drop uses, gives zero distortion but cannot blend into perspective. To fake it, use a very long lens (5–8° at 15–20 m) and dolly-zoom into the stroke camera.

Render the cue, aim line and ghost ball in 3D from **preallocated meshes moved by transform and uniforms**, never `ImmediateMesh` or per-frame `ArrayMesh` rebuilds. The aim line is a 1 cm QuadMesh 1 mm above the cloth, with world-space dashes (3 cm on, 2 cm off) from `fract(UV.x·length/period)`, so it foreshortens correctly in the stroke camera. The ghost ball is either a flat ring quad, which is cheapest and never z-fights, or a rim-alpha sphere reusing the house GHOST_SHADER pattern. The cue is a tapered 12-segment CylinderMesh of about 30 vertices, banded in the shader, with shadows off. Spin, power and HUD stay in 2D.

## Conclusion

The hardest part of Cue Club is the CPU's compute budget, not the physics. Closed-form phases make the solver exact and cheap enough to run live, but GDScript makes every rollout expensive. The research gives the way out: difficulty lives in execution noise, and lookahead and sample counts add surprisingly little. An analytic pot-probability estimate can therefore replace most of the simulation the plan assumed. The same estimate, run at the human's likely noise level, also shows why small physics choices become UX problems. Pocket tolerances on medium shots are a few tenths of a degree, while throw can reach several degrees and squirt more than one, so any physical effect the guideline does not show reads as unfairness. The guideline should therefore show the solver's own prediction, and the effects it cannot show (squirt) stay off.

The open risks are empirical: GDScript solver speed on the test phone, whether `sin`/`exp` agree bit-for-bit between desktop and Android, the MSAA cost on the phone's Adreno GPU, whether number labels are legible at about 20 dp, and how long a casual rack actually lasts. Each can be measured in the first week of building `shared/cue_sim`. The microbenchmark and two golden files should come before any tuning, because the CPU budget, the test tolerances and the choice between constant and speed-dependent friction all depend on those measurements.
